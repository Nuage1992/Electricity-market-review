注记[P001] Matamala et al. (2025) — A Bi-Level Nash Bargaining Model for Electricity Trading Among Microgrids With Endogenous Nodal Prices

Paper: Yolanda Matamala, Kevin A. Melendez, and Felipe Feijoo
Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 16(4), 3113–3124, 2025
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2025.3570964

Main topics: multi-microgrid trading; Nash bargaining; Stackelberg game; endogenous prices; locational marginal price; battery flexibility; renewable generation

Quick takeaway

This paper asks a simple but important question:

If several microgrids trade electricity with each other, how can they set a fair trading price when their actions also change electricity prices in the main grid?

The paper combines two ideas:

  1. A Nash Bargaining Solution (NBS) is used to share the economic benefit fairly among microgrids.
  2. A Stackelberg game is used to model the interaction between the microgrids and the Independent System Operator (ISO).

The key point is that both the trading price among microgrids and the grid’s Locational Marginal Price (LMP) are treated as endogenous prices. They are determined by the model instead of being fixed inputs.

1. Problem addressed

There are two linked problems.

Problem A — Cooperation may reduce total cost but still be unfair

A group of microgrids can cooperate and minimize their total operating cost.

However, this does not mean every microgrid benefits.

One microgrid may receive most or all of the cost reduction, while another microgrid receives little or no benefit. If a microgrid cannot benefit individually, it has little reason to join the trading scheme.

Problem B — Grid prices are affected by microgrid decisions

Many trading models treat the electricity price from the main grid as a fixed input.

This paper argues that this is incomplete.

When microgrids buy from or sell to the main grid, they change the net demand seen by the grid. This changes generator dispatch and congestion. As a result, the LMP can also change.

Therefore:

microgrid trading decisions affect grid prices, and grid prices affect microgrid trading decisions.

The two sides need to be solved together.

2. Why existing approaches are not enough

The paper highlights two common approaches.

Total-cost or social-welfare optimization

This can find a low-cost solution for the whole group.

The weakness is that it does not guarantee a fair distribution of the benefit among individual microgrids.

Nash bargaining models

Nash bargaining can improve fairness.

However, many previous bargaining studies do not fully capture the feedback between:

  • trading prices among microgrids,
  • LMPs in the main grid,
  • buying and selling decisions,
  • storage operation, and
  • renewable generation.

This paper tries to connect all of these decisions in one model.

3. Core idea

The proposed framework has two levels.

Upper level — Microgrids

The microgrids cooperate with each other.

They decide:

  • how much electricity to buy or sell,
  • how to use renewable generation,
  • how to charge or discharge batteries,
  • how much electricity to exchange with the main grid, and
  • the trading price among microgrids.

The trading relationship is modeled using the Nash Bargaining Solution (NBS).

Lower level — Main grid

The Independent System Operator (ISO) operates the main grid.

The ISO solves a Direct Current Optimal Power Flow (DC-OPF) problem.

Its objective includes:

  • electricity generation cost, and
  • the Social Cost of Carbon (SCC) associated with generator emissions.

The lower-level model produces the Locational Marginal Price (LMP) at each grid node.

4. Why this is a bi-level problem

The microgrids are treated as the leaders.

The ISO is treated as the follower.

The logic is:

  1. Microgrids make trading and operating decisions.
  2. These decisions change the power injected into or withdrawn from the grid.
  3. The ISO redispatches generation.
  4. The LMP changes.
  5. The new LMP changes the cost of buying or selling electricity for the microgrids.

So the upper and lower levels are strongly connected.

This is the main reason for using a Stackelberg bi-level model.

5. What does “endogenous price” mean here?

An endogenous price is a price calculated inside the model.

It is not given in advance.

This paper has two important endogenous prices:

  • the trading price among microgrids, and
  • the LMP from the main grid.

This is one of the main contributions of the paper.

It allows the model to study how renewable generation, storage, electricity trading, and grid operation jointly affect prices.

6. How fairness is defined

The most useful concept in this paper is the disagreement point.

The disagreement point is the cost of a microgrid when cooperation or peer trading is not available.

For microgrid \(m\):

  • \(d_m\): disagreement-point cost,
  • \(z_m\): cost after cooperation.

The benefit from cooperation is therefore:

\[ d_m-z_m \]

If this value is positive, the microgrid saves money by cooperating.

The basic Nash bargaining idea is to maximize the product of these benefits:

\[ \max \prod_m (d_m-z_m) \]

This is different from only minimizing:

\[ \sum_m z_m \]

The first expression cares about how the benefit is distributed.

The second mainly cares about the total cost.

This difference explains why Nash bargaining can produce a fairer result.

7. How the disagreement point is obtained

The disagreement point is not assumed.

The authors calculate it by solving the market when microgrids do not trade electricity with each other.

This non-cooperative case is formulated as an Equilibrium Problem with Equilibrium Constraints (EPEC).

The resulting cost for each microgrid becomes its disagreement point.

This gives the bargaining model a clear baseline:

A microgrid should not accept cooperation if it performs worse than it would without cooperation.

8. Model solution

The original bi-level model is difficult to solve directly.

The authors convert it into a Mathematical Program with Equilibrium Constraints (MPEC).

The lower-level DC-OPF is replaced by its Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions.

This creates complementarity constraints and nonlinear terms.

Several techniques are then used to improve solution performance:

  • Special Ordered Set Type 1 (SOS1) for complementarity constraints,
  • Second-Order Cone (SOC) reformulation for the Nash bargaining objective,
  • strong duality for some bilinear terms, and
  • a valid inequality to improve the optimization bound.

The numerical results show that SOS1 performs much better than a standard Big-M formulation for the tested cases.

9. Case study

The model is tested on:

  • a PJM 5-bus system with two microgrids, and
  • an IEEE 14-bus system with four microgrids.

The time horizon is 24 hours.

The microgrids include:

  • electricity demand,
  • wind and solar generation,
  • battery storage, and
  • connection to the main grid.

Different renewable-generation levels and battery capacities are tested.

10. Main findings

Renewable generation reduces market prices

When renewable generation increases, the microgrids need less electricity from fossil-fuel generators in the main grid.

In the high-renewable scenario:

  • the nodal price decreases by about 1.53%, and
  • the trading price among microgrids decreases by about 1.27%.

This shows an important feedback:

more local renewable generation can influence not only microgrid operation but also the market price seen by the microgrids.

Cooperation reduces dependence on the main grid

For the microgrid with lower generation, bargaining reduces electricity purchased from the ISO by about:

  • 3% in the low-renewable scenario, and
  • 10% in the high-renewable scenario.

Battery storage makes trading more flexible

A microgrid can buy electricity when its nodal price is low, charge its battery, and later sell electricity when prices are higher.

Storage therefore changes both the timing and economic value of electricity trading.

Nash bargaining improves fairness

The grand-coalition approach and the bargaining approach can produce a similar total cost reduction.

But the distribution can be very different.

For the high-generation case reported in the paper:

  • total cost reduction: about $1,788,
  • grand coalition: the benefit is allocated to only one microgrid,
  • Nash bargaining: about $884 for one microgrid and $904 for the other.

This is the clearest result of the paper.

The main value of Nash bargaining is therefore not necessarily a larger total saving.

It is a more acceptable distribution of the saving.

11. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Minimum total cost is not the same as fair cooperation

This is important in systems with independent owners.

A mathematically optimal solution for the whole system may be unacceptable to an individual participant.

Lesson 2 — The baseline matters

The disagreement point gives each participant a reference.

The question is not only:

Is the cooperative system cheaper?

It is also:

Is each participant better off than without cooperation?

Lesson 3 — Electricity prices should not always be treated as fixed inputs

If distributed resources become large enough, their actions can affect grid dispatch and nodal prices.

In that case, price and operation should be modeled together.

Lesson 4 — Storage is not only an energy-balancing device

Battery storage also creates market flexibility.

It allows a microgrid to move electricity across time and respond to price differences.

12. What remains unsolved

Stated by the authors

The authors suggest studying oligopolistic behavior and market manipulation.

For example, large microgrids may strategically change their interaction with the ISO to influence network prices.

Mitigation strategies are therefore an important future topic.

Further limitations visible from the model setup

These are useful research questions that are not fully addressed in this paper:

  • Renewable generation is tested through scenarios, but explicit forecast uncertainty is not modeled in the bargaining problem.
  • The lower-level network uses DC-OPF, so voltage, reactive power, and detailed AC network behavior are not considered.
  • The test systems contain only two or four microgrids.
  • The optimization can still require hours to solve as the problem becomes larger.
  • The framework assumes that microgrids can cooperate and exchange the information needed for joint bargaining.
  • The model focuses on one market period structure rather than repeated strategic behavior over many market days.

13. Research idea triggers

Uncertainty

Can Nash bargaining be combined with:

  • stochastic optimization,
  • robust optimization, or
  • chance constraints

to handle renewable, load, and price uncertainty?

Scalability

Can the centralized model be replaced by a distributed or decomposition-based algorithm for tens or hundreds of microgrids?

Real-time operation

Can the same idea be used in rolling-horizon or real-time trading instead of solving a large day-ahead problem?

Network model

What changes if AC power flow, voltage constraints, or distribution-network constraints are included?

Market power

Can some microgrids manipulate the endogenous LMP?

How should market power be detected or limited?

Fairness

How would the result change if another benefit-allocation rule were used instead of Nash bargaining?

Joint planning and operation

Can storage sizing, renewable sizing, and electricity trading be optimized together?

Multi-energy systems

Can the framework include heat, hydrogen, electric vehicles, or flexible loads?

注记[P002] Gao et al. (2023) — A Blockchain Peer-to-Peer Energy Trading System for Microgrids

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(5), 3944–3960, 2023
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2023.3237624
Main topics: blockchain; Peer-to-Peer (P2P) energy trading; security; privacy; energy theft; microgrids

Quick takeaway

This paper focuses on how to make P2P energy trading secure and trustworthy.

Its main concern is not how to optimize electricity prices or trading quantities. Instead, it asks:

How can prosumers trade electricity without relying on one central database, while protecting transaction data from tampering, privacy leakage, and attacks?

1. Problem addressed

P2P energy trading requires frequent communication among prosumers and consumers.

This creates several risks:

  • transaction data may be changed or leaked;
  • smart-meter data may be manipulated;
  • users may collude or report false energy information;
  • centralized trading platforms create a single point of failure.

The paper therefore develops a blockchain-based trading platform with additional security and theft-detection mechanisms.

2. Proposed solution

The framework combines four main parts:

  1. Private Ethereum blockchain
    Trading records are stored in an immutable distributed ledger.

  2. Certificateless Signcryption (CLSC)
    Encryption and digital signing are combined in one process to protect confidentiality, authenticity, and integrity.

  3. Leader election and consensus
    Processing nodes elect a leader and use a consensus process to validate transactions before adding them to the blockchain.

  4. Smart-contract energy theft detection
    Reported energy use is compared with supplied energy. Large mismatches are treated as possible energy theft.

The main entities are prosumers/consumers, an Energy Request Aggregator, a Data Processing Center, and a trusted Certificate Authority (CA).

3. How trading works

A simplified process is:

energy request → request aggregation → buyer selects seller → encrypted/signed transaction → blockchain validation → payment and power transfer

The Energy Request Aggregator collects requests and displays trading information.

The CA manages keys and authentication.

The blockchain processing nodes validate transactions and store them after consensus.

4. Main results

The proposed signcryption scheme has low computational cost because it avoids expensive bilinear pairing operations.

The reported execution times are:

  • signcryption: 1.94 ms
  • unsigncryption: 3.88 ms

The authors report lower computation and communication costs than the four comparison schemes.

Blockchain experiments were also carried out on the Ethereum Ropsten network.

For leader election, UDP broadcast performs better than standard TCP and point-to-point UDP because fewer messages are needed.

5. What this paper teaches

The main lesson is:

P2P energy trading is not only an optimization problem. It is also a communication, trust, privacy, and cybersecurity problem.

Blockchain can provide a tamper-resistant transaction record, but blockchain alone is not enough. Authentication, encryption, consensus, and abnormal-energy-use detection are also required.

This paper is therefore more about the digital infrastructure behind energy trading than the economic design of the market.

6. What remains unsolved

Important limitations are:

  • The framework does not optimize trading prices or energy quantities.
  • Power-flow and network operating constraints are not included in the trading mechanism.
  • The system still relies on a fully trusted CA and an Energy Request Aggregator.
  • Validation is mainly based on cryptographic analysis and blockchain/network simulation rather than a real microgrid deployment.
  • Larger real-time systems may still face latency, throughput, and transaction-cost problems.

7. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • combining blockchain security with energy-market optimization or game theory;
  • including power-flow constraints in blockchain-based P2P trading;
  • designing a more decentralized identity and authentication mechanism;
  • adding privacy-preserving market clearing;
  • testing the platform with real microgrid and smart-meter data;
  • combining cyberattack detection with physical power-system anomaly detection.

注记[P003] Azim et al. (2023) — A Cooperative P2P Trading Framework: Developed and Validated Through Hardware-in-Loop

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(4), 2999–3015, 2023
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2022.3225520
Main topics: Peer-to-Peer (P2P) trading; cooperative game; network constraints; Shapley value; Software-in-the-Loop (SIL); Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL)

Quick takeaway

The paper addresses a practical gap in P2P trading:

A financially attractive trade may still be unsafe for the distribution network.

The proposed framework therefore links:

  • a financial layer for P2P trading and benefit allocation;
  • a physical network layer for checking voltage and branch-flow limits;
  • a real-time validation layer using SIL and HIL.

1. Problem addressed

Most P2P studies mainly focus on one of two sides:

  • financial design: price, trading quantity, benefit allocation;
  • physical network operation: voltage, congestion, losses.

The problem is that a trade can be economically attractive but create voltage violations or excessive power flow in a low-voltage network.

A second gap is validation. Many network-aware P2P methods are only tested by numerical simulation, so their readiness for real implementation is unclear.

2. Why existing approaches are not enough

The paper groups existing work into three broad types:

  1. Financial-layer studies
    Good at market design, but often ignore physical network limits.

  2. Network studies without full coordination
    They analyse network impacts after trading decisions are made.

  3. Coordinated financial–network studies
    They consider both layers, but are usually validated only in software.

The missing part is:

coordinated P2P trading + network safety + hardware-based validation.

3. Overall framework

The main logic is:

  1. Prosumers decide intended P2P trading quantities.
  2. A P2P Market Operator (PMO) checks whether these quantities are compatible with the distribution network.
  3. If there is no violation, the trade is accepted.
  4. If there is a violation, the trading quantity is reduced and checked again.
  5. The final economic benefit is then evaluated and allocated.

The financial decisions remain with the prosumers. The PMO only checks physical feasibility.

4. Financial layer: cooperative trading

The financial layer uses a Canonical Coalition Game (CCG).

All participating prosumers form a grand coalition and cooperate to reduce their total electricity cost.

The model determines:

  • P2P selling quantity;
  • P2P buying quantity;
  • P2P trading price;
  • electricity exchanged with the grid.

The P2P price is kept between the Feed-in Tariff (FiT) and the grid Time-of-Use (ToU) price.

This creates an incentive for both sides:

  • sellers earn more than the FiT;
  • buyers pay less than the retail ToU price.

5. Benefit allocation: Shapley value

The total coalition benefit is not simply shared equally.

The paper uses the Shapley value.

The idea is:

a prosumer receives a benefit according to its marginal contribution to the coalition.

This is used to keep the coalition fair and stable.

The authors also prove that the proposed coalition satisfies the required cooperative-game properties, including grand-coalition formation and stability.

6. Physical network constraint checking

The physical layer checks whether P2P power injections violate the distribution network.

The main constraints are:

  • bus voltage limits;
  • branch power-flow limits.

An AC network model is used for the actual validation.

If a proposed P2P injection causes a violation, the framework reduces the trading quantity rather than completely blocking the prosumer.

Example:

  • intended injection: 6.69 kW
  • voltage limit is violated;
  • allowed injection after correction: 5 kW
  • reduction: 25.26%

This is a key practical idea of the paper:

find the maximum safe trading quantity instead of simply rejecting the trade.

7. SIL and HIL validation

The authors validate the method using both:

  • Software-in-the-Loop (SIL);
  • Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL).

The platform combines MATLAB, RSCAD, and a Real-Time Digital Simulator (RTDS).

The HIL setup includes:

  • PV emulator;
  • PV inverter;
  • battery and inverter;
  • programmable load;
  • resistive load;
  • power amplifier.

The purpose is to check whether the P2P algorithm still behaves correctly when connected to realistic hardware.

8. Case study

The test system is a typical Australian low-voltage distribution network.

Key settings:

  • 35 buses;
  • 102 residential customers;
  • 30 prosumers participate in P2P trading;
  • ToU price: 17–35 ¢/kWh;
  • FiT: 10 ¢/kWh.

Different residential load types are also considered using ZIP load models.

9. Main findings

Network safety

The proposed framework keeps voltage and branch-flow limits within acceptable ranges by adjusting trading quantities when needed.

SIL versus HIL

The voltage difference between SIL and HIL results is reported to be less than 1%.

This supports the practical validity of the simulation framework.

Economic benefit

All participating prosumers reduce their daily electricity costs compared with Business-as-Usual (BAU).

Reported cost reductions range from:

9.65% to 47.03%

The proposed method also performs better than an existing CCG method that does not directly internalize the network constraints.

10. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Economic feasibility is not enough

A P2P trade must also satisfy physical network limits.

Lesson 2 — Network constraints should be part of the trading process

Checking the network only after finalizing the market decision can lead to infeasible trades.

Lesson 3 — Do not block more power than necessary

If a trade causes a violation, reducing the quantity can be better than rejecting the trade completely.

Lesson 4 — HIL is an important step toward real deployment

A useful research path is:

simulation → SIL → HIL → field test

11. What remains unsolved

The paper still leaves several open issues:

  • battery dynamics are simplified within each simulation step;
  • battery degradation is not modeled;
  • privacy and data encryption are not fully developed;
  • the PMO remains a centralized network-checking entity;
  • uncertainty in PV, load, and prices is not deeply considered;
  • scalability for very large numbers of prosumers is not studied in detail;
  • HIL is closer to practice, but it is still not a full field deployment.

12. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • distributed network-feasibility checking without a central PMO;
  • stochastic or robust P2P trading under PV/load uncertainty;
  • battery degradation and inter-temporal battery constraints;
  • privacy-preserving P2P trading;
  • large-scale real-time coordination;
  • blockchain + cooperative game + network constraints;
  • HIL validation of more advanced P2P market designs.

注记[P004] Zhang et al. (2023) — A Coordinated Multi-Energy Trading Framework for Strategic Hydrogen Provider in Electricity and Hydrogen Markets

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(2), 1403–1417, 2023
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2022.3154611
Main topics: hydrogen trading; electricity market; Stackelberg game; auction pricing; biomass electrolysis; stochastic optimization; ancillary services

Quick takeaway

This paper studies a Hydrogen Provider (HP) that participates in both the hydrogen and electricity markets.

The key question is:

How can an HP coordinate hydrogen production, hydrogen sales, and grid-support services so that it earns more while using renewable energy more efficiently?

The framework combines market trading, hydrogen production, storage, and ancillary services in one system.

1. Problem addressed

The paper targets three linked problems.

Green hydrogen is still expensive

Conventional water electrolysis consumes a large amount of electricity. This reduces the economic value of renewable-to-hydrogen conversion.

A hydrogen provider may not fully use its assets

Hydrogen demand changes during the day. During low-demand periods, hydrogen storage and conversion equipment may be underused.

Fixed hydrogen prices do not reflect buyer response

Many hydrogen-trading studies use a fixed hydrogen price. This ignores how Hydrogen Fueling Stations (HFSs) change their purchase quantities when the price changes.

The paper therefore tries to coordinate:

renewable energy → hydrogen production → hydrogen trading → electricity-market services

2. Why existing approaches are not enough

Previous studies often focus on only one part of the problem:

  • green hydrogen production;
  • hydrogen-market trading;
  • strategic bidding;
  • electricity-hydrogen coupling.

The authors identify two important gaps:

  1. hydrogen pricing often does not consider the buyers’ willingness to pay;
  2. the HP’s ability to earn from both hydrogen sales and electricity ancillary services is not fully used.

The paper also argues that conventional water electrolysis has relatively low conversion efficiency, especially when renewable power fluctuates.

3. Overall electricity-hydrogen trading framework

The main participants are:

  • Hydrogen Provider (HP);
  • Hydrogen Fueling Stations (HFSs);
  • hydrogen auctioneer;
  • electricity market / Independent System Operator (ISO).

The HP uses wind, solar, and biomass to produce green hydrogen.

The produced hydrogen can be:

  • sold to HFSs;
  • stored for later use;
  • converted back to electricity through a fuel cell;
  • used indirectly to provide reserve services to the power grid.

The electricity and hydrogen markets are cleared separately, but the HP coordinates its operation across both markets.

4. Hydrogen pricing: auction + Stackelberg game

The hydrogen price is not fixed.

The paper develops a Vickrey auction-based pricing mechanism to screen eligible buyers and define a reasonable price range.

Then a single-leader-multiple-follower Stackelberg game is used.

  • Leader: hydrogen auctioneer;
  • Followers: HFSs.

The logic is simple:

  1. the auctioneer announces a hydrogen price;
  2. each HFS chooses how much hydrogen to buy;
  3. the auctioneer updates the price;
  4. the process continues until no player wants to change its decision.

The final point is the Stackelberg Equilibrium (SE).

This allows the hydrogen price and trading amount to reflect buyer demand instead of being imposed as fixed values.

5. How the HP makes stacked profit

The HP has two main income sources.

Hydrogen market

It sells green hydrogen to HFSs.

Electricity market

It provides ancillary services, mainly operating reserves.

Typical operation is:

  • during high H2 demand, hydrogen is mainly sold to HFSs;
  • during low H2 demand, stored hydrogen and spare equipment capacity can be used for electricity-market services.

For up-reserve, stored hydrogen can be converted to electricity through a fuel cell.

For down-reserve, the electrolyzer can absorb additional electricity and convert it to hydrogen when spare hydrogen-storage capacity is available.

The main idea is to use the same assets in more than one market.

6. Hybrid renewable-to-hydrogen production

The paper does not use only conventional water electrolysis.

It considers biomass electrolysis.

The key idea is to replace the difficult oxygen-evolution reaction in water electrolysis with biomass oxidation.

This can reduce the required electrolysis voltage and improve hydrogen-production efficiency.

The HP includes:

  • Wind Turbine (WT);
  • Photovoltaic-Thermal unit (PVT);
  • electrolyzer;
  • Hydrogen Storage Tank (HST);
  • battery;
  • fuel cell;
  • electric boiler.

Solar thermal energy and recovered heat are also used to raise the electrolyzer temperature and improve the hydrogen-production process.

7. Role of the battery and hydrogen storage

The Hydrogen Storage Tank (HST) shifts hydrogen between different time periods.

This allows the HP to:

  • store excess hydrogen;
  • meet later HFS demand;
  • keep hydrogen available for electricity-market services.

The battery has a different role.

Renewable power can change quickly. Large current fluctuations can accelerate electrolyzer degradation.

The battery therefore helps smooth the electrolyzer current.

So the two storage devices have different functions:

HST → energy shifting and market flexibility

Battery → fast power smoothing and electrolyzer protection

8. Uncertainty and optimization

The paper considers uncertainty in:

  • wind generation;
  • solar generation;
  • HFS demand-related utility parameters.

A scenario-based stochastic programming method is used.

The study first generates 10,000 scenarios and then reduces them to 100 representative scenarios to lower the computational burden.

The optimization maximizes the HP’s expected daily profit while considering:

  • hydrogen sales revenue;
  • up/down reserve revenue;
  • battery degradation cost;
  • electrolyzer operation and degradation cost;
  • hydrogen storage limits;
  • battery limits;
  • electrolyzer temperature/current limits;
  • hydrogen-pipeline constraints.

9. Case study

The test system contains:

  • one HP;
  • four HFSs;
  • 3 MW wind capacity;
  • 3.5 MW PVT capacity;
  • a hydrogen storage tank;
  • battery, electrolyzer, fuel cell, and boiler.

A 24-hour day-ahead operation is studied.

Four schemes are compared:

  1. proposed coordinated framework;
  2. hydrogen trading only;
  3. fixed hydrogen price;
  4. conventional alkaline water electrolysis-based Power-to-Gas (P2G).

10. Main findings

Multi-market participation increases profit

Compared with hydrogen trading only, the proposed framework increases the HP’s daily profit by:

22.05%

The reason is that the HP also earns from electricity reserve services.

Auction pricing improves hydrogen trading

Compared with the fixed-price method, hydrogen sales revenue increases by:

2.25%

The auction-based mechanism also improves the combined utility of the HFSs.

Biomass electrolysis improves renewable utilization

Compared with the conventional Alkaline Electrolysis (AEL)-based P2G method:

  • average electrolysis efficiency improves by 25.87%;
  • wind-solar accommodation improves by 8.17%.

The iterative game is reasonably scalable

For 4 to 40 HFSs, the proposed Stackelberg-game algorithm converges within at most 37 iterations in the reported tests.

11. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — One energy asset can participate in several markets

Hydrogen equipment does not need to earn money only by selling hydrogen.

The same system can also provide flexibility to the electricity market.

Lesson 2 — Price and demand should interact

A fixed hydrogen price misses an important market response:

price changes demand, and demand should influence price.

Lesson 3 — Storage technologies can have different roles

Battery storage and hydrogen storage are not interchangeable.

They operate on different time scales and solve different problems.

Lesson 4 — Production technology affects market performance

The market model and the physical hydrogen-production model are connected.

Higher conversion efficiency changes how much renewable energy can be used and how profitable the trading strategy becomes.

12. What remains unsolved

Several assumptions leave room for further research:

  • only one strategic HP is considered;
  • the HP is assumed to be a price-taker in the electricity market;
  • electricity and hydrogen markets are cleared independently;
  • hydrogen delivery is based on pipeline transport assumptions;
  • larger competitive hydrogen markets are not modeled;
  • uncertainty is handled by scenarios, which can become computationally expensive;
  • practical deployment and experimental validation are not included.

The authors specifically identify multiple competing HPs and HFSs as an important future direction.

13. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • multiple competing HPs using game theory or equilibrium models;
  • coordinated clearing of electricity and hydrogen markets;
  • market power of large hydrogen providers;
  • robust or distributionally robust optimization instead of scenario-based stochastic programming;
  • detailed hydrogen-network dynamics and compressor operation;
  • long-term sizing plus short-term market operation;
  • battery and electrolyzer degradation-aware trading;
  • real-time or rolling-horizon hydrogen trading;
  • carbon pricing and green-hydrogen certification;
  • HIL or pilot-scale validation of electricity-hydrogen market coordination.

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(4), 2572–2581, 2023
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2022.3232283
Main topics: smart building; microgrid; Solar Photovoltaic (SPV); Battery Energy Storage (BES); Energy Management Scheme (EMS); real-time tariff; peak demand; power quality

Quick takeaway

This paper develops a real-time rule-based Energy Management Scheme (EMS) for a grid-connected smart building with solar PV and battery storage.

The main idea is simple:

charge the battery when grid electricity is cheap, avoid excessive grid demand, and use or sell stored energy when electricity is expensive.

Unlike Model Predictive Control (MPC) or mathematical optimization, the EMS does not require load/PV forecasts or repeated online optimization.

The paper also adds a separate converter-control method to improve grid-side power quality.

1. Problem addressed

The paper tries to solve four practical problems at the same time:

  1. reduce the building’s electricity cost;
  2. avoid Maximum Demand Charges (MDC) caused by excessive grid power;
  3. keep backup energy available and improve microgrid reliability;
  4. maintain good grid-side Power Quality (PQ) under nonlinear loads and distorted grid voltage.

The key challenge is to achieve these functions in real time without a computationally heavy optimizer.

2. Why existing approaches are not enough

The authors point out several limitations of existing EMS methods.

MPC and optimization-based EMS

They can produce good schedules, but often require:

  • forecasts of PV and load;
  • accurate future electricity prices;
  • repeated online optimization;
  • higher computation time.

Forecast errors can also reduce the quality of the scheduled solution.

Existing rule-based EMS

Rule-based methods are much simpler, but many do not explicitly consider:

  • real-time tariff changes;
  • maximum demand limits;
  • peak-demand management;
  • power quality.

The proposed method tries to keep the simplicity of rule-based control while adding these practical functions.

3. Microgrid structure

The system contains:

  • rooftop SPV;
  • Battery Energy Storage (BES);
  • local smart-building loads;
  • utility grid;
  • bidirectional battery converter;
  • Voltage Source Converter (VSC).

The EMS mainly decides how much power should come from the grid and how the battery should charge or discharge.

4. Core idea: real-time rule-based EMS

The EMS uses only current system information.

Main inputs are:

  • load demand \(P_L\);
  • PV generation \(P_{PV}\);
  • battery State of Charge (SOC);
  • real-time grid tariff \(C_g(t)\);
  • maximum allowed grid power \(P_{GMAX}\).

The control decision is therefore based on:

current tariff + current load/PV balance + current battery SOC

rather than a forecasted future trajectory.

5. Three tariff periods and their control logic

The electricity price is divided into three conditions.

Off-peak period

Grid electricity is cheap.

Main action:

charge the battery from the grid, provided the battery has enough remaining capacity.

The purpose is to store cheap electricity for later use.

Normal-price period

The battery normally remains in a floating state.

PV is first used for the local load. Surplus PV can be exported.

If grid demand approaches the maximum limit, the battery can provide power leveling.

Peak-price period

Grid electricity is expensive.

Main action:

discharge the battery to reduce grid purchase or export power to the grid.

This allows the building to benefit from high electricity prices.

6. Peak demand and maximum demand charge

The EMS does not only follow electricity price.

It also enforces a grid-power limit:

\[ -P_{GMAX} < P_G < P_{GMAX} \]

If the building demand would push grid power above the allowed limit, the battery discharges to reduce the grid demand.

This helps avoid the Maximum Demand Charge (MDC).

If the battery SOC is already too low and the grid limit would still be exceeded, the original rule set may require load shedding.

This is an important practical limitation of the method.

7. Role of the battery

The battery has several functions:

  • store cheap off-peak electricity;
  • store excess PV generation;
  • discharge during high-price periods;
  • reduce peak grid demand;
  • provide short-term backup during disturbances.

The battery is therefore used for both:

energy-cost arbitrage and power-leveling support.

Its charging/discharging rate and SOC are limited for safe operation.

8. Power quality control: VGESOGI-FLL

The EMS handles energy and cost.

A second control layer handles power quality.

The paper proposes a Variable Gain Enhanced Second-Order Generalized Integrator Frequency-Locked Loop (VGESOGI-FLL) for the VSC.

Its purpose is to:

  • remove dominant current harmonics;
  • reject DC offsets;
  • track grid-frequency changes;
  • keep grid currents balanced and nearly sinusoidal;
  • provide reactive-power support;
  • operate the grid side close to unity power factor.

The controller changes its filter gains during dynamic conditions to obtain faster response.

9. Simulation and hardware validation

The method is tested in both simulation and laboratory hardware.

The studies include:

  • summer and winter operating profiles;
  • distorted grid voltage;
  • load unbalance;
  • a 5 Hz grid-frequency jump;
  • voltage sag;
  • nonlinear loads.

For the 5 Hz frequency jump, the Frequency-Locked Loop (FLL) tracks the new frequency within about 40 ms.

The hardware prototype uses a solar simulator, BES, bidirectional converter, VSC, and a dSPACE 1103 controller.

10. Main findings

Energy management

In both summer and winter comparisons, the proposed EMS has:

  • the lowest net energy absorbed from the grid;
  • the highest reported net profit/cost saving among the compared EMS schemes.

For the summer case, the reported values are:

  • net average grid energy: 2 kWh/day;
  • net average profit plus cost saving: Rs 540/day.

Power quality

In hardware tests, one grid-voltage phase has a Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) of 8.91%.

The VSC control keeps the grid-current THD within the 5% limit referenced from IEEE Std 519.

11. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Simple rules can be useful when fast real-time decisions are needed

Not every EMS needs MPC, Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP), or another heavy optimizer.

A rule-based controller can be attractive when:

  • computation must be very fast;
  • forecasts are poor or unavailable;
  • the operating logic is easy to define.

Lesson 2 — Tariff arbitrage and peak-demand management are different objectives

A battery can earn value by shifting energy across tariff periods, but it can also reduce maximum grid demand and avoid demand penalties.

Lesson 3 — Energy management and power quality are separate control problems

The EMS determines how much power should flow.

The VSC controller determines how that power should be exchanged with acceptable waveform quality.

This distinction is useful when designing smart-building microgrids.

12. Limitations

The paper itself identifies several limitations:

  • decisions depend strongly on accurate real-time tariff information;
  • battery SOC estimation must be accurate;
  • communication or estimation delays can lead to wrong EMS decisions;
  • some operating states require load shedding.

Additional limitations visible from the framework are:

  • the EMS follows predefined rules rather than solving a future-horizon optimization problem;
  • future PV, load, and price information is intentionally not used;
  • battery degradation is not explicitly included in the economic objective;
  • the paper does not study uncertainty in a formal stochastic or robust framework.

So the method gains simplicity and speed, but gives up some of the future-looking capability of predictive optimization.

13. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • adaptive rules that update automatically from historical operation;
  • MPC or Economic MPC (EMPC) using the rule-based EMS as a benchmark;
  • battery degradation-aware tariff control;
  • stochastic or robust control under PV/load uncertainty;
  • demand-response flexibility instead of load shedding;
  • real-time electricity pricing instead of predefined tariff bands;
  • coordinated building thermal loads, EVs, and battery storage;
  • learning-based EMS with hard safety constraints;
  • combining economic EMS and power-quality objectives in one coordinated framework.

注记[P006] Li et al. (2025) — A Cost-Effective Online Dynamic Scheduling Method for Communication Services in Large-Scale Software-Defined Virtual Power Plants

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 16(6), 5641–5654, 2025
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2025.3581975
Main topics: Virtual Power Plant (VPP); Software-Defined Networking (SDN); communication cost; P95 billing; Quality of Service (QoS); online scheduling; routing optimization

Quick takeaway

This paper studies a problem that is often ignored in VPP research:

communication itself costs money, especially when a VPP connects hundreds or thousands of distributed sites.

The paper develops an online routing method that reduces communication cost while still meeting the required Quality of Service (QoS).

Its key idea is to combine:

  • an offline optimization database;
  • online matching of current traffic patterns to historical patterns;
  • a fast routing algorithm for the current time slot.

1. Problem addressed

Large-scale VPPs depend on continuous communication between:

  • the cloud control center;
  • distributed energy resources;
  • controllable loads;
  • storage systems;
  • electric vehicles;
  • utility-grid and electricity-market interfaces.

Building a dedicated private network for all sites is expensive.

A practical solution is to rent existing communication links, such as:

  • low-cost Internet;
  • high-reliability dedicated lines such as Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS).

The problem is:

how should the VPP route different communication services across these links so that total communication cost is minimized without violating latency, jitter, and packet-loss requirements?

2. Why existing approaches are not enough

Most VPP studies focus on the energy side:

  • economic dispatch;
  • market participation;
  • renewable forecasting;
  • storage scheduling;
  • frequency regulation.

Communication research usually focuses on:

  • delay;
  • reliability;
  • cybersecurity;
  • communication protocols.

The economic cost of communication is much less studied.

Another issue is that many cost-minimizing traffic methods assume the entire future communication demand over the billing cycle is already known.

That assumption is unrealistic for real-time VPP operation.

3. SD-VPP communication architecture

The paper uses a Software-Defined Virtual Power Plant (SD-VPP) architecture.

There are two layers.

Device layer

Includes photovoltaic and wind resources, controllable loads, energy storage, and electric vehicles.

Resources in the same area are grouped as one VPP site.

Network layer

The network connects all VPP sites to the cloud control center.

It uses Software-Defined Networking (SDN) so that routing can be changed dynamically.

The topology is mainly hub-and-spoke:

VPP site → network provider → VPP cloud control center

4. Three types of communication services

The paper classifies communication services by their QoS requirements.

High real-time services

Examples:

  • real-time control;
  • protection;
  • security-event monitoring.

These require very low latency and high reliability, so they must use dedicated MPLS links.

Near real-time services

Examples:

  • monitoring data;
  • equipment management;
  • electricity-market signals.

These also mainly use MPLS links.

Non-real-time services

Examples:

  • metering and settlement data;
  • equipment logs;
  • image/video data.

These can tolerate more delay and may use either MPLS or Internet links.

The key point is:

different VPP data should not all use the same communication path.

5. Why P95 billing matters

A key concept in the paper is P95 billing.

During a billing cycle, traffic is measured at fixed intervals, for example every 5 minutes.

The traffic values are sorted, and the highest 5% are ignored.

The next highest value becomes the billing bandwidth.

So the VPP can temporarily use high bandwidth in about 5% of the time slots without increasing the billed bandwidth.

This creates a scheduling opportunity:

use the “free” 5% peak slots carefully to reduce total network cost.

The difficulty is that the cost of the current time slot depends on traffic over the whole billing cycle.

6. Offline scheduling model

The authors first build an offline optimization model.

The objective is to minimize:

  • fixed communication cost;
  • variable bandwidth cost under P95 billing.

The model also considers:

  • link capacity;
  • QoS;
  • upstream/downstream traffic;
  • routing choices;
  • link-sharing relationships.

The original problem is a Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programming (MINLP) model.

It is reformulated into a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) model so it can be solved with Gurobi.

The main purpose is to create an offline database of good routing patterns for later online use.

7. IORS and DORS for offline database construction

For small systems, the paper uses Ideal Optimal Routing Strategies (IORS).

IORS solves the full MILP and gives high-quality offline solutions.

For large systems, memory and computation become too expensive.

The authors therefore introduce Decoupled Optimal Routing Strategies (DORS).

DORS separates branch-site subproblems and solves them independently.

It gives up some global optimality, but makes large-scale database construction practical.

This is a clear engineering trade-off:

slightly less optimal → much easier to solve.

8. Online CEOR algorithm

The main online method is called Cost-Effective Online Rolling (CEOR).

Each billing cycle is divided into:

  • past period;
  • current decision period;
  • future period.

At each time slot, CEOR performs two main steps.

Step 1 — Match the current traffic pattern

Features are extracted from recent communication traffic.

These features are compared with records in the offline database.

The best-matching historical record is selected, and its future traffic pattern is used as an estimate of coming traffic.

Step 2 — Make the current routing decision

Using:

  • already observed traffic;
  • estimated future traffic;
  • current QoS conditions;

the algorithm quickly selects the routing strategy for the current time slot.

Then the process rolls forward to the next time slot.

9. What makes CEOR fast

The paper avoids solving a large MILP from scratch every 5 minutes.

Instead, CEOR:

  1. reuses information from the offline database;
  2. estimates the P95 billing bandwidth from past and matched future traffic;
  3. checks a limited set of feasible routing options;
  4. prioritizes larger-demand sites first.

Its practical complexity is approximately:

\[ O(TN\log N) \]

where \(T\) is the number of time slots and \(N\) is the number of branch sites.

10. Case study

The study uses real VPP-related communication data.

Main settings include:

  • 4 operator-network nodes;
  • 10 to 1000 branch sites;
  • 3 service types;
  • 30-day billing cycle;
  • 5-minute scheduling interval;
  • 8640 decision time slots per billing cycle.

CEOR is compared with:

  • Load Balancing (LB);
  • Cascara (CA);
  • IORS/DORS;
  • Single Time Slot Gurobi (STSG).

11. Main findings

Communication cost

For large-scale systems, CEOR consistently reduces communication cost.

In the reported large cases, CEOR reduces total cost by roughly:

6%–40% compared with other algorithms

depending on system size and the comparison method.

For some cases, savings relative to LB exceed 50%.

Computational speed

This is the strongest result.

Compared with STSG, CEOR is typically 2–3 orders of magnitude faster.

For 100 branch sites:

  • STSG maximum online decision time: 312.96 s;
  • CEOR: about 3.93 s.

For 1000 branch sites:

  • CEOR still completes one decision in about 17.23 s.

This is well below the 5-minute decision interval.

Scalability

STSG runs into memory problems for very large systems.

CEOR remains computationally feasible.

So the main value of CEOR is not only lower cost, but also:

real-time scalability.

12. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Communication cost is part of VPP operating cost

A VPP is not only an energy optimization system.

Large-scale VPPs also need communication infrastructure, and this can become economically important.

Lesson 2 — Communication services have different priorities

Control and protection signals should not be treated in the same way as logs, images, or settlement data.

QoS requirements should affect routing decisions.

Lesson 3 — Billing rules can create optimization opportunities

The P95 rule means that temporary traffic peaks may not increase the bill.

A smart scheduler can exploit this feature.

Lesson 4 — Offline optimization can support fast online decisions

Instead of solving a large optimization problem from scratch every time, historical optimized solutions can be reused to guide online decisions.

13. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • future traffic is approximated by matching historical patterns;
  • online performance depends on the quality and diversity of the offline database;
  • DORS sacrifices some optimality to improve scalability;
  • the study is numerical and does not yet include an SDN testbed;
  • network failures and cyberattacks are not the main focus;
  • communication routing is not directly co-optimized with VPP energy dispatch.

The authors identify SDN testbed implementation and large-scale practical validation as future work.

14. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • joint optimization of energy dispatch + communication routing;
  • communication-aware VPP economic dispatch;
  • adaptive or learning-based traffic prediction instead of historical feature matching;
  • robust routing under link failures and cyberattacks;
  • communication-cost-aware frequency regulation;
  • edge/cloud computation placement together with routing;
  • dynamic communication pricing instead of fixed P95 rules;
  • multi-timescale coordination between fast communication control and slower VPP energy scheduling;
  • HIL or SDN testbed validation.

注记[P007] Li et al. (2023) — A Data-Driven Joint Chance-Constrained Game for Renewable Energy Aggregators in the Local Market

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(2), 1430–1440, 2023
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2022.3163131
Main topics: Local Energy Market (LEM); Renewable Energy Aggregator (REA); Cournot competition; overbidding risk; joint chance constraint; Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO); Wasserstein ambiguity set; Nash equilibrium

Quick takeaway

This paper studies how Renewable Energy Aggregators (REAs) should bid in a Local Energy Market when renewable generation is uncertain.

The central problem is:

an REA wants to bid more to earn more, but renewable generation may be lower than the bid, creating overbidding and power-shortfall risk.

The paper combines:

  • a competitive quantity-bidding game;
  • distribution-network constraints;
  • a joint chance constraint for overbidding risk;
  • a data-driven Wasserstein ambiguity set when the true probability distribution is unknown.

1. Problem addressed

The paper focuses on a one-hour-ahead Local Energy Market.

Each REA aggregates excess renewable energy and submits a bid.

The main difficulty is that the REA does not know exactly how much renewable power will be available.

If:

\[ \text{bid} > \text{actual available renewable energy} \]

the REA cannot fully deliver its promised energy.

This is called overbidding.

The market therefore needs to balance two competing goals:

higher bid → potentially higher profit

lower bid → lower delivery risk

2. Why existing approaches are not enough

Price-taker models

A large REA may have enough market power to influence the local clearing price.

Treating every REA as a price taker can therefore miss strategic competition.

Scenario-based stochastic methods

These methods use sampled renewable-generation scenarios.

However:

  • many samples may be required;
  • decisions can depend strongly on the selected scenarios;
  • good in-sample performance does not guarantee good out-of-sample risk control.

Classical chance constraints

These usually require the probability distribution of the uncertain renewable output to be known or assumed.

In practice, the true distribution may be unknown.

The paper therefore asks:

Can we control bidding risk using only a limited amount of historical data without assuming a known probability distribution?

3. Local market and Cournot competition

The market contains:

  • Renewable Energy Aggregators (REAs);
  • Load Aggregators (LAs);
  • Local Market Operator (LMO);
  • Bulk Power System (BPS).

The market process is:

  1. LAs report demand.
  2. REAs submit energy bids.
  3. The market is cleared under network and risk constraints.
  4. The local clearing price is published.
  5. Any remaining imbalance can be exchanged with the bulk power system.

The REAs compete in quantity, so the paper uses a Cournot game.

The clearing price is:

\[ p=\alpha-\beta\sum_i x_i \]

where \(x_i\) is the bid of REA \(i\).

This means:

more total renewable bids → lower market price.

So one REA’s bidding decision affects the profits of the other REAs.

4. Core risk: renewable overbidding

Each REA has an uncertain maximum renewable supply.

The paper requires that all REAs can simultaneously deliver their bids with sufficiently high probability:

\[ P(\tilde{\xi}_i \ge x_i,\ \forall i) \ge 1-\epsilon \]

This is a joint chance constraint.

The important word is joint:

the model does not only control each REA’s risk separately.

It controls the probability that the whole set of bids can be delivered together.

The study uses a target risk tolerance of 10% in its main cases.

5. Why the method is “data-driven distributionally robust”

The true probability distribution of renewable output is assumed to be unknown.

Only finite historical samples are available.

The paper builds an empirical distribution from these samples and then considers many possible probability distributions around it.

These possible distributions form a Wasserstein ambiguity set.

The idea is:

Do not trust one estimated probability distribution completely.
Make the bidding decision safe for a family of plausible distributions around the observed data.

This is a form of Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO).

6. Meaning of the Wasserstein radius

The parameter \(\delta\) controls the size of the ambiguity set.

Small \(\delta\)

The model stays close to the empirical data.

Result:

  • less conservative bidding;
  • potentially higher bids;
  • higher sensitivity to the training samples.

Large \(\delta\)

More probability distributions are considered possible.

Result:

  • more conservative bids;
  • lower overbidding risk;
  • stronger robustness.

So \(\delta\) controls an important trade-off:

profit opportunity ↔︎ robustness

7. Network constraints are included

The bids are not cleared independently of the power network.

The paper includes a linearized AC distribution-network model based on LinDistFlow.

The market-clearing constraints include:

  • active-power balance;
  • reactive-power balance;
  • voltage limits;
  • line apparent-power limits.

This means an economically attractive bid is still rejected if it violates the distribution network.

8. Game equilibrium and solution method

Each REA maximizes its own profit.

The profit contains:

  • market revenue;
  • aggregation cost.

Because the players share network and chance constraints, this is a Generalized Nash Equilibrium Problem (GNEP) rather than a standard Nash game.

The paper proves that a generalized Nash equilibrium exists.

A best-response seeking algorithm based on the Nikaido-Isoda function is then used to find a normalized Nash equilibrium.

The basic logic is:

  1. start from a feasible bidding strategy;
  2. find a better response;
  3. update the bids;
  4. repeat until the changes become sufficiently small.

9. Case study

Two radial distribution systems are tested:

  • modified IEEE 33-node network with 4 REAs;
  • modified IEEE 69-node network with 8 REAs.

For the main synthetic-data study:

  • 1000 renewable-output samples are generated;
  • 50 samples are used for training;
  • the remaining samples are used for out-of-sample testing.

The paper also validates the model using historical wind and PV data from the Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO).

10. Effect of the Wasserstein radius

In the 33-node case, increasing \(\delta\) from 0.01 to 0.07 reduces the bids of REA1–REA4 by:

  • 38%
  • 68%
  • 27%
  • 26%

The total renewable bid decreases, and the clearing price increases from:

97.60 $/MWh → 108.28 $/MWh

The less reliable REA reduces its bid more strongly.

More reliable REAs can retain more of their bids and may earn higher profit.

This means the risk-control mechanism naturally favors participants that can deliver energy more reliably.

11. Risk-control performance

A major result is that increasing the Wasserstein radius reduces the out-of-sample overbidding rate.

For the main 33-node and 69-node studies, the overbidding rate remains below the target 10% risk tolerance.

The paper also shows that very small training datasets can be dangerous.

For example, with only 10 training samples in the 69-node case:

  • training overbidding rate: 0%
  • testing overbidding rate: nearly 12%

So a model can appear perfectly safe on training data but still fail on unseen data.

This is an important warning for data-driven market models.

12. Comparison with CVaR-based stochastic game

The benchmark uses Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) in a scenario-based stochastic game.

A risk-weight parameter \(\gamma\) must be manually selected to balance:

  • profit;
  • overbidding risk.

The proposed Wasserstein-based method instead puts the risk requirement directly in the feasible set.

For the main comparison with \(\delta=0.03\):

  • proposed method training overbidding rate: 0%
  • proposed method testing overbidding rate: 3.6%

The reported testing risk is lower than the compared CVaR cases.

The paper therefore concludes that the proposed method provides better risk-averse and out-of-sample performance.

13. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Renewable bidding is a profit-risk problem

Bidding more is not always better.

An unreliable REA should bid more conservatively.

Lesson 2 — Competition changes the uncertainty problem

Each REA is not optimizing alone.

Its bid changes:

  • the market price;
  • competitors’ incentives;
  • network conditions.

Lesson 3 — Historical data do not define the true distribution perfectly

A model built only around one empirical distribution can be overconfident.

The Wasserstein ambiguity set adds protection against distribution errors.

Lesson 4 — Robustness has a price

A larger ambiguity set reduces risk but also reduces bidding opportunities.

Choosing the robustness level is therefore an economic decision, not only a mathematical one.

14. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • the Wasserstein radius must still be selected by the modeler;
  • the inner approximation of the joint chance constraint can introduce conservatism;
  • the local market uses a specific linear Cournot pricing function;
  • renewable uncertainty is represented mainly through maximum available supply;
  • transmission loss and ancillary services are not included in the market design;
  • the model is validated numerically rather than through a real market experiment.

The authors identify improved chance-constraint approximation, transmission losses, and ancillary services as future work.

15. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • adaptive or data-driven selection of the Wasserstein radius;
  • Distributionally Robust Optimization with correlated PV, wind, load, and price uncertainty;
  • multi-period bidding with storage and inter-temporal constraints;
  • battery-enabled REAs that use storage to reduce overbidding risk;
  • strategic market participation of multiple VPPs instead of only renewable aggregators;
  • joint energy and reserve-market bidding;
  • network-aware peer-to-peer or local market trading;
  • comparison of Wasserstein DRO, stochastic optimization, robust optimization, and chance-constrained methods under the same data;
  • learning-based bidding combined with hard chance constraints;
  • real-time or rolling-horizon market clearing.

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(2), 1388–1402, 2023

Main topics: Carbon Emission Rights (CER); decentralized microgrid; electricity trading; energy storage; distributed optimization; Tracker-Based Distributed Algorithm (TBDA)

Quick takeaway

This paper asks:

How can a microgrid trade electricity and carbon emission rights at the same time, without relying on a central market coordinator?

The proposed model combines:

  • local electricity trading;
  • Carbon Emission Rights (CER) trading;
  • Energy Storage (ES);
  • a microgrid-wide carbon-emission cap;
  • a fully distributed optimization algorithm.

The key idea is that users optimize locally and exchange information only with neighbors, while the whole microgrid still reaches power balance and satisfies the total carbon limit.

1. Problem addressed

A low-carbon microgrid needs to consider both:

  • economic operation: users want low electricity cost and trading profit;
  • carbon responsibility: high-emission generators should face a cost for their emissions.

A simple carbon-emission constraint can limit emissions, but it does not create a market incentive for users to trade and reduce emissions continuously.

The paper therefore introduces CER trading into a decentralized electricity market.

A second problem is coordination.

Many distributed methods still need a central coordinator to update global information. This creates concerns about:

  • information privacy;
  • market manipulation;
  • dependence on a central agent.

The paper aims to remove this central layer.

2. Decentralized electricity–carbon market

The microgrid contains different types of users, including:

  • micro-gas-turbine generators;
  • photovoltaic generators;
  • wind generators;
  • general prosumers and consumers.

Users can:

  • generate electricity;
  • buy or sell electricity;
  • charge or discharge storage;
  • buy or sell CER.

There is no central trading institution.

Each user solves its own local problem and exchanges limited information with neighboring users.

3. How CER trading works

A generator receives an initial amount of CER.

Its daily carbon emission depends on its electricity generation.

If actual emissions exceed its CER

The generator first buys additional CER from the market.

If CER supply is still insufficient, it pays an emission penalty.

If actual emissions are below its CER

The unused CER can be sold to other generators.

The basic logic is:

low-emission generator → surplus CER → sell CER

high-emission generator → insufficient CER → buy CER / pay penalty

The whole microgrid must also satisfy a total carbon-emission cap.

So there are two levels of carbon control:

  1. individual CER balance;
  2. microgrid-wide emission limit.

4. Electricity trading and pricing

Users can also buy and sell electricity inside the microgrid.

The paper uses a Mid-Market Rate (MMR) pricing method.

The local trading price is set between:

  • the price for selling surplus electricity to the external grid;
  • the retail price for buying electricity from the grid.

This creates incentives for local trading:

  • sellers can receive more than the external feed-in price;
  • buyers can pay less than the retail grid price.

The model minimizes the total user cost, including:

  • generation cost;
  • electricity purchase cost;
  • electricity sales revenue;
  • CER purchase/sales cost;
  • carbon-emission penalties.

5. Role of energy storage

Energy Storage (ES) is included for every user in the case study.

It helps users:

  • shift electricity across time;
  • reduce purchases during expensive periods;
  • store energy during low-demand periods;
  • respond to peak demand.

The model includes:

  • charging/discharging limits;
  • State of Charge (SOC) limits;
  • charging/discharging efficiency;
  • final energy reserve requirement.

An interesting result is that the authors prove that the optimal solution does not need an additional binary constraint to prevent simultaneous charging and discharging.

Because of storage losses and the cost structure, simultaneous charging and discharging cannot be optimal.

This keeps the model convex and avoids adding extra binary variables.

6. Why a fully distributed algorithm is needed

Traditional centralized optimization can solve the joint electricity-carbon problem.

However, a central coordinator would collect large amounts of private information and continuously update global variables.

The authors argue that this may create:

  • privacy concerns;
  • dependence on the scheduling center;
  • risk of information manipulation.

The paper therefore develops a Tracker-Based Distributed Algorithm (TBDA).

Each user calculates locally and communicates only with neighbors.

7. Four types of constraints in TBDA

The model contains four different types of constraints.

Local Inequality Constraints (LIC)

Examples:

  • generator limits;
  • trading limits;
  • battery limits;
  • CER trading limits.

These belong to one user.

Local Equality Constraints (LEC)

Examples:

  • local power balance;
  • individual CER accounting.

Global Equality Constraints (GEC)

Examples:

  • total electricity bought = total electricity sold;
  • total CER bought = total CER sold.

These require balance across all users.

Global Inequality Constraints (GIC)

The main example is:

  • total microgrid carbon emissions must remain below the carbon cap.

Handling both global equality and global inequality constraints without a central coordinator is one of the main algorithmic contributions.

8. Tracker-Based Distributed Algorithm

The basic process is:

  1. classify and reconstruct the different constraints;
  2. each user solves its own local optimization problem;
  3. local trackers estimate the status of global constraints;
  4. neighboring users exchange tracker information;
  5. decision variables and multipliers are updated;
  6. the process repeats until global balance is achieved.

The tracker allows each user to estimate system-wide information without directly obtaining all users’ private data.

The authors also provide a convergence analysis.

9. Case study

The test microgrid contains:

  • 14 users;
  • 6 prosumers;
  • 8 consumers;
  • distributed storage for all users;
  • both physical power connections and communication links.

The scheduling horizon is:

  • 24 hours;
  • 1-hour time steps.

The distributed TBDA result is compared with a centralized solution obtained using Gurobi.

10. Main findings

Electricity and CER markets reach balance

The electricity market converges after about 356 iterations.

Final electricity bought and sold:

1937.8 kWh

The CER market reaches equilibrium faster, after about 120 iterations.

Final CER bought and sold:

0.17 kg

Carbon-emission cap is satisfied

Total microgrid carbon emission:

3.21 kg

Emission limit:

3.5 kg

Compared with the case without the global carbon constraint, total carbon emissions are reduced by:

4.8%

Distributed result is close to the centralized optimum

Total cost:

  • TBDA: $101.89
  • centralized Gurobi solution: $101.28

The difference is below 1%.

This shows that the fully distributed method can achieve a result close to centralized optimization.

11. How CER changes generator dispatch

This is one of the most useful results of the paper.

A generator with cheap electricity but high carbon emissions may lose its economic advantage after CER costs are included.

For example:

  • User 4 has the lowest generation cost: 0.218 $/kWh;
  • but it has high unit carbon emissions;
  • it sells only 167.9 kWh.

In contrast, User 3 has a higher generation cost but very low carbon emissions.

It sells:

371.5 kWh

So CER trading changes the dispatch rule from:

only generation cost matters

to:

generation cost + carbon performance matter together

This is the main mechanism by which the carbon market changes microgrid operation.

12. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Carbon limits and carbon markets are different

A carbon cap only says how much can be emitted.

A CER market also creates an economic signal that changes individual generator behavior.

Lesson 2 — Low-cost generation is not always the preferred generation

When carbon has a price, a cheap but dirty generator may become less competitive.

Lesson 3 — Carbon and electricity can be traded together

Electricity trading handles physical energy balance.

CER trading handles the economic responsibility for emissions.

They are different products but influence the same dispatch decisions.

Lesson 4 — Distributed does not always mean fully decentralized

Many “distributed” methods still use a central coordinator.

This paper specifically tries to remove that layer by using local trackers and neighbor-to-neighbor communication.

13. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • carbon price and penalty parameters are predefined;
  • uncertainty in renewable generation and load is not explicitly modeled;
  • the market uses a simple MMR electricity-pricing mechanism rather than strategic price formation;
  • network power-flow constraints are simplified compared with a full AC distribution-network model;
  • all users are assumed to communicate through a connected and reliable communication graph;
  • communication delay, packet loss, and cyberattacks are not considered;
  • validation is numerical rather than based on HIL or a field microgrid.

14. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • stochastic or Distributionally Robust Optimization for renewable and load uncertainty;
  • dynamic CER prices instead of fixed carbon prices;
  • game-theoretic electricity and carbon price formation;
  • battery degradation-aware electricity-carbon trading;
  • AC-network-constrained decentralized carbon trading;
  • communication failures and asynchronous distributed optimization;
  • privacy-preserving or encrypted TBDA;
  • electricity + CER + green certificate joint markets;
  • carbon-aware Virtual Power Plant (VPP) operation;
  • rolling-horizon carbon-aware microgrid Energy Management Systems (EMS).

注记[P009] Cao and Chen (2026) — A Differentially Private Energy Trading Mechanism Approaching Social Optimum

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 17(2), 1444–1456, 2026
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2025.3628290
Main topics: Peer-to-Peer (P2P) energy trading; differential privacy; generalized Nash game; privacy attack; Laplacian noise; Nash equilibrium; social optimum

Quick takeaway

This paper focuses on a question that is easy to overlook in distributed energy trading:

If prosumers repeatedly exchange information to reach a market equilibrium, can an attacker infer their private electricity demand from those messages?

The answer is yes.

The paper first shows that a traditional distributed Nash-equilibrium algorithm can leak private demand information.

It then adds carefully calibrated Laplacian noise to the distributed updates so that the mechanism provides differential privacy while still converging, in expectation, close to the original Nash equilibrium.

1. Problem addressed

In a P2P energy market, prosumers repeatedly exchange coordination variables to decide:

  • how much energy to self-produce;
  • how much to buy or sell;
  • what bid to submit.

These iterative messages can reveal more information than expected.

The paper treats each prosumer’s electricity demand \(d_i\) as sensitive information because it can reflect:

  • occupancy;
  • appliance use;
  • daily habits;
  • consumption patterns.

The core problem is therefore:

how to coordinate strategic prosumers without exposing private demand data.

2. Why existing privacy approaches are not enough

The paper discusses three common privacy technologies.

Homomorphic encryption

It can calculate directly on encrypted data.

Advantage:

  • strong data confidentiality.

Weakness:

  • high computational burden;
  • limited supported operations.

Secret sharing

Private data are split into multiple shares.

Advantage:

  • no single party sees the full data.

Weakness:

  • higher communication and coordination overhead.

Blockchain

Blockchain can improve transaction integrity and traceability.

However:

  • pseudonymity is not the same as privacy;
  • transparent transaction records may still support inference attacks;
  • computation can be expensive.

The paper therefore focuses on Differential Privacy (DP) because it provides a quantifiable privacy guarantee and can be implemented by controlled noise injection.

3. P2P market and Nash game

Each prosumer must satisfy:

\[ p_i+q_i=d_i \]

where:

  • \(p_i\): self-production;
  • \(q_i\): energy bought from or sold to the P2P market;
  • \(d_i\): electricity demand.

The trading quantity is linked to the market price through:

\[ q_i=-a\lambda+b_i \]

where \(b_i\) is the prosumer’s bid and \(a\) is the market sensitivity.

The market price is obtained from the clearing condition:

\[ \sum_i q_i=0 \]

Each prosumer minimizes its own production and trading cost.

Because all participants share the same market-clearing condition, the original problem is formulated as a generalized Nash game.

4. Why traditional distributed equilibrium seeking leaks privacy

The traditional algorithm lets each prosumer maintain estimates of the other prosumers’ bids.

During iteration, these estimates are exchanged with neighbors.

The paper develops an adversarial inference model to show that these exchanged trajectories can reveal private demand.

In the worst-case setting:

  • the attacker knows the private parameters of all but one prosumer;
  • the attacker observes several iterations of the target prosumer’s coordination variables.

The authors prove that the remaining prosumer’s private demand can then be recovered exactly under the stated conditions.

In the six-prosumer example, the target demand is:

15 kWh

and the attack can recover this value accurately using only a small number of observed iterations.

This is the key privacy warning of the paper:

distributed does not automatically mean private.

5. Differential privacy mechanism

To protect the private demand, the paper adds Laplacian noise to the distributed equilibrium update.

Instead of using the exact private parameter \(\beta_i\), the algorithm uses:

\[ \beta_i+\gamma_i \]

where:

\[ \gamma_i \sim \text{Laplace}(0,2\sigma_\gamma^2) \]

The noise scale \(\sigma_\gamma\) controls the privacy strength.

The paper proves that the algorithm satisfies \(\epsilon\)-differential privacy when the noise level is sufficiently large.

The general relationship is:

larger noise → smaller \(\epsilon\) → stronger privacy

but also:

larger noise → larger deviation from the exact market equilibrium

So the key design problem is:

privacy ↔︎ economic performance

6. What differential privacy means here

Differential privacy does not mean that the attacker sees no data.

Instead, it means that the observable algorithm output changes only slightly when one participant’s private demand changes.

Therefore, the attacker cannot confidently determine whether a specific private demand value was present in the original dataset.

A smaller privacy parameter \(\epsilon\) gives stronger privacy protection.

This is useful because the privacy level becomes a measurable design parameter, rather than a vague statement that the algorithm is “secure.”

7. Privacy attack results

The target demand is 15 kWh.

When the noise level increases, the inferred demand becomes more dispersed.

The percentage of inferred values within ±10% of the true demand drops from:

  • 78.7% at \(\sigma_\gamma=1\)
  • 55.7% at \(\sigma_\gamma=2\)
  • 17.9% at \(\sigma_\gamma=10\)
  • 7.3% at \(\sigma_\gamma=20\)
  • 3.9% at \(\sigma_\gamma=100\)

So stronger noise makes accurate inference much more difficult.

8. More attack data does not always help the attacker

An interesting result is that giving the attacker more iteration data does not always improve inference accuracy.

With \(\sigma_\gamma=2\), the percentage of inferred values within ±5% of the true demand first rises and then falls:

  • 100 observed iterations: 29.0%
  • 200 iterations: 35.4%
  • 500 iterations: 24.7%
  • 700 iterations: 18.2%
  • 1000 iterations: 17.0%

The authors explain this as a balance between:

  • more observations helping the attacker;
  • accumulated noise making those observations less informative.

This is a useful reminder that privacy attacks depend on both data quantity and data quality.

9. Convergence and equilibrium quality

The paper proves that, with a suitable step size, the proposed algorithm converges in expectation to the Nash equilibrium.

This does not mean every single noisy trajectory reaches exactly the same equilibrium point.

Instead:

  • individual outcomes fluctuate because of noise;
  • the average converged result approaches the original Nash equilibrium.

Larger noise generally makes the iteration more uncertain and initially slows convergence.

However, all tested noise levels still converge.

10. Privacy–utility trade-off

The main cost of privacy is reduced fidelity.

If the noise is very small:

  • the result is very close to the original Nash equilibrium;
  • privacy protection is weak.

If the noise is very large:

  • privacy is strong;
  • bids and total cost can deviate significantly.

The paper measures this using:

  • total cost gap;
  • equilibrium deviation.

A particularly interesting result is that moderate noise can occasionally produce a lower total cost than the original Nash equilibrium.

The reason is that random perturbation can partly offset inefficient strategic behavior among self-interested prosumers.

This does not mean “more noise is always better.”

When noise becomes too large, market efficiency deteriorates.

11. Market sensitivity and social optimum

The parameter \(a\) represents how strongly traded energy responds to the market price.

The paper shows that increasing \(a\) makes the Nash equilibrium closer to the social optimum.

However, larger \(a\) also increases the number of iterations needed for convergence.

So another trade-off appears:

higher economic efficiency ↔︎ slower convergence

This suggests that market parameters can be designed not only for pricing, but also for equilibrium quality and computational performance.

12. Scalability

The paper tests systems with:

  • 6;
  • 10;
  • 20;
  • 50;
  • 100 prosumers.

The number of iterations increases strongly with system size.

For example:

  • 6 prosumers: 4892 iterations
  • 100 prosumers: 84377 iterations

However, the reported computation time remains below 0.5 s because the algorithm is distributed and each local update is simple.

The authors therefore argue that the method is computationally scalable for practical market-clearing intervals.

13. Network constraints

The main theoretical model neglects detailed distribution-network constraints.

The authors explain that their focus is the privacy–efficiency trade-off.

A separate numerical study is then carried out on a 69-node distribution network with six flexible prosumers.

With moderate Laplacian noise, the bidding process remains stable while network constraints are enforced.

The paper therefore shows that the privacy mechanism can still work under network constraints, but the full theoretical formulation with network constraints remains future work.

14. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Information exchange can reveal private data

Even if users never directly share their electricity demand, repeated algorithm messages may allow an attacker to reconstruct it.

Lesson 2 — Distributed is not the same as privacy-preserving

A market can be decentralized and still leak sensitive information.

Lesson 3 — Privacy has an economic cost

Adding noise protects users, but also changes bidding decisions and market efficiency.

Lesson 4 — Noise can sometimes improve social efficiency

A moderate perturbation may reduce the negative effect of strategic behavior and move some outcomes closer to the social optimum.

This is an unusual and interesting finding.

15. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • only electricity demand is protected; other private parameters are not;
  • the main theoretical model does not include network constraints;
  • the market is static rather than multi-period;
  • energy storage and inter-temporal decisions are not included;
  • the adversarial model focuses on a specific inference structure;
  • communication delay, packet loss, and malicious message injection are not considered;
  • privacy protection is based on adding noise, so the privacy–utility trade-off cannot be eliminated.

16. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • differential privacy for multi-period P2P trading;
  • privacy-preserving energy trading with battery storage;
  • network-constrained generalized Nash games with formal privacy guarantees;
  • dynamic privacy budgets that change with system state;
  • differential privacy + blockchain;
  • differential privacy + federated learning;
  • privacy-aware Virtual Power Plant (VPP) coordination;
  • adaptive noise design based on attack risk;
  • joint privacy and cybersecurity against inference and false-data attacks;
  • mechanism design that optimizes privacy + social welfare + convergence speed together.

注记[P010] Zhang et al. (2023) — A Framework for Several Electricity Retailers Cooperatively Implement Demand Response to Distributed Data Center

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(1), 277–289, 2023
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2022.3189835
Main topics: Distributed Data Center Cluster (DDCC); Demand Response (DR); workload shifting; electricity retailers; cooperative game; Shapley value; Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP)

Quick takeaway

This paper studies a coordination problem created by geographically distributed data centers.

A Distributed Data Center Cluster (DDCC) may contain data centers in different regions, and each data center may buy electricity from a different retailer.

The key problem is:

If each retailer independently asks its local data center to increase or reduce electricity use, workload shifting inside the DDCC may help one retailer but unintentionally hurt another.

The paper therefore proposes a cooperative framework in which:

  1. several retailers jointly send DR instructions;
  2. the DDCC shifts workloads to maximize the total profit of all participants;
  3. the final profit is redistributed using the Shapley value so that cooperation remains attractive to every participant.

1. Problem addressed

A DDCC can move computing workloads among geographically separated data centers.

This flexibility is valuable for Demand Response (DR), because electricity use can be moved from one region to another without stopping the computing service.

However, different data centers may be supplied by different electricity retailers.

If the retailers act independently, one retailer’s DR request may create an unwanted effect for another retailer.

Example:

  • Retailer 1 wants Data Center 1 to reduce electricity use.
  • The DDCC moves part of its workload to Data Center 2.
  • Data Center 2 therefore consumes more electricity.
  • If extra electricity sales are unfavorable for Retailer 2, Retailer 2 loses money.

So the real problem is not simply:

How can a data center reduce load?

It is:

How should several retailers coordinate the spatial shifting of one common customer’s load?

2. Why independent DR can be inefficient

The important feature of a DDCC is workload spatial shifting.

A workload removed from one data center usually has to be processed somewhere else.

Therefore:

\[ \text{load reduction in one region} \]

often means:

\[ \text{load increase in another region} \]

If retailers issue DR requests separately, they only see their own local benefit.

They may not know whether the workload is shifted to a region where the corresponding retailer benefits or loses.

The paper’s Fig. 1 gives a simple three-region example:

  • Retailer 1 benefits from reducing Data Center 1 load;
  • Retailer 2 is harmed if Data Center 2 load increases;
  • Retailer 3 benefits if Data Center 3 load increases.

Cooperation allows the workload to be moved from Data Center 1 to Data Center 3 rather than Data Center 2.

3. Two-step cooperation framework

The framework has two main steps.

Step 1 — Maximize total cooperative profit

The electricity retailers and the DDCC form an ED union.

The retailers jointly send their DR requirements to the DDCC.

The DDCC then redistributes workloads among its data centers to maximize:

retailers’ total DR profit + DDCC electricity-cost saving

Step 2 — Reallocate the profit fairly

Maximizing total profit does not guarantee that every participant benefits individually.

Some retailer may lose money even when the whole group gains.

The paper therefore applies the Shapley value to redistribute the total cooperative profit.

The target is:

every participant receives at least as much profit as under independent operation.

4. What information does each retailer send?

Each retailer sends two types of DR information.

Economic effect of load change

The retailer calculates:

  • profit change if the local data center reduces 1 unit of electricity;
  • profit change if the local data center increases 1 unit of electricity.

These values depend on:

  • day-ahead market purchase;
  • real-time market price;
  • retail electricity price;
  • possible DR reward or penalty.

Allowed load-change range

The retailer also tells the DDCC how much the local data-center load is allowed to increase or decrease.

The limit considers:

  • maximum permissible demand deviation;
  • local distribution-network capacity.

So the DDCC receives both:

economic preference + feasible adjustment range

from every retailer.

5. DDCC energy-management model

After receiving all DR instructions, the DDCC decides how to distribute computing workloads.

Main decision variables include:

  • workload assigned from each front-end server to each data center;
  • number of active servers in each data center;
  • resulting electricity consumption of each data center.

The optimization objective is:

\[ \max \; \text{total profit of the ED union} \]

The model is formulated as a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) problem.

6. Workload shifting is limited by computing-service quality

The DDCC cannot move workloads only according to electricity economics.

The computing service must still satisfy delay limits.

The model therefore considers:

  • workload transmission delay between regions;
  • processing delay at each data center;
  • server service rate;
  • maximum number of available servers.

Processing delay is modeled using M/M/1 queueing theory.

This creates an important coupling:

electricity-side DR decisions ↔︎ computing-side Quality of Service

A data center cannot simply deactivate servers or receive unlimited workloads if the resulting computing delay becomes too large.

7. How workload determines electricity consumption

The logic is:

workload allocation → active server number → server utilization → data-center electricity use

An active server consumes power between its idle and peak power.

The total IT electricity is then multiplied by the Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) to obtain the total data-center electricity consumption.

Therefore, the paper does not directly set electricity consumption as a free variable.

Electricity use is linked to the actual computing workload.

8. Profit allocation: why Shapley value is needed

The Shapley value allocates cooperative profit according to each member’s marginal contribution.

This is important because:

maximum total profit ≠ fair individual profit

After the collaborative DR is completed, some participants may initially earn less than under independent operation.

The paper introduces a common fund pool:

  • some participants pay money into the pool;
  • others receive money from the pool;
  • the total transfer sums to zero.

The total cooperative profit is unchanged.

Only its distribution changes.

This gives each member a reason to remain in the cooperation.

9. Why traditional local incentive allocation can be unfair

The paper compares the Shapley-based allocation with a traditional approach where each retailer rewards its own local data center according to local load reduction.

This can be misleading in a DDCC.

For example:

  • Data Center 2 reduces its local load;
  • its workload is shifted to Data Center 3;
  • Retailer 3 may be the largest beneficiary;
  • but Retailer 2 is still required to reward Data Center 2.

In the paper’s comparison, this traditional allocation can even make Retailer 2’s DR profit negative.

The reason is that:

local load reduction does not necessarily identify who actually benefits from the workload shift.

10. Case study

The case contains:

  • 4 electricity retailers;
  • 4 geographically distributed data centers;
  • 100,000 servers in each data center;
  • a 24-hour operating setting;
  • workload data from a public data-center archive;
  • electricity-price data from PJM.

The MILP is solved using Gurobi 9.0.

Reported computation time is about:

0.5 s

Four main operating situations are compared:

  • S1: no DR;
  • S2: one retailer independently implements DR;
  • S3: collaborative DR + proposed Shapley allocation;
  • S4: collaborative DR + traditional profit allocation.

11. How collaborative DR changes workload allocation

Under independent operation, the DDCC mainly sends more workloads to data centers with lower electricity prices.

When only Retailer 1 requests DR, workloads removed from Data Center 1 are spread to other data centers without considering whether the other retailers benefit.

Under collaborative DR:

  • Data Centers 1 and 2 reduce electricity use;
  • Data Centers 3 and 4 increase electricity use.

This matches the different economic preferences of all four retailers.

The key improvement is therefore not only load reduction.

It is:

moving computing load to the regions where that movement creates the highest total value.

12. Main findings

The collaborative scheme produces a higher total profit than the independent DR cases.

More importantly, after Shapley-value redistribution:

  • every electricity retailer benefits;
  • the DDCC benefits;
  • no participant receives less than under independent operation.

The paper also shows that the proposed profit-allocation mechanism is more suitable than rewarding each local data center only according to its local load reduction.

13. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Flexible load can create cross-region externalities

Reducing electricity use at one location may increase electricity use somewhere else.

Therefore, local DR decisions can affect other market participants.

Lesson 2 — Data-center workloads are an energy-flexibility resource

A DDCC can change regional electricity demand by moving computing tasks without necessarily reducing total computing service.

Lesson 3 — System-wide optimization and benefit allocation are different problems

First determine the best cooperative action.

Then decide how the benefit should be shared.

Both steps are necessary for stable cooperation.

Lesson 4 — Energy optimization should respect computing constraints

Electricity savings cannot come at the cost of unacceptable processing delay.

This is a typical energy-computing coupling problem.

14. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • only workload spatial shifting is modeled in detail;
  • workload processing-time shifting is not included;
  • renewable generation at data centers is not considered;
  • the DDCC is assumed to buy electricity from retailers rather than directly from wholesale markets;
  • uncertainty in electricity price and workload forecasts is not explicitly modeled;
  • retailer cooperation is assumed rather than strategically negotiated;
  • the Shapley value may become computationally expensive when the number of participants becomes large.

The authors specifically identify workload time shifting, direct wholesale-market participation, and data-center renewable generation as future extensions.

15. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • joint spatial + temporal workload shifting;
  • DDCC participation in wholesale electricity and ancillary-service markets;
  • renewable-powered and battery-equipped data centers;
  • stochastic or robust optimization under workload and electricity-price uncertainty;
  • carbon-aware workload migration across regions;
  • electricity price + carbon intensity + computing delay co-optimization;
  • game-theoretic cooperation among multiple retailers;
  • scalable alternatives to Shapley-value profit allocation;
  • data-center DR coordinated with Virtual Power Plants (VPPs);
  • demand response for AI / GPU data-center clusters;
  • multi-timescale scheduling linking fast computing control with slower energy-market decisions.

注记[P011] Luo et al. (2026) — A Hierarchical Game-Decoupled-RL-Based Energy Flow Optimization Strategy for Cross-Regional Railway Integrated Energy System

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 17(4), 2646–2659, 2026
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2026.3655819
Main topics: Railway Integrated Energy System (RIES); Regenerative Braking Energy (RBE); Energy Storage System (ESS); photovoltaic generation; game theory; Q-learning; hierarchical energy management; real-time control

Quick takeaway

This paper studies how several railway traction substations can coordinate regenerative braking energy, photovoltaic energy, and energy storage across regions.

The main difficulty is that a wide-area Railway Integrated Energy System (RIES) has too many coupled control variables for one centralized real-time controller.

The proposed solution is a two-level hierarchical Energy Management System (EMS):

  1. Upper level: use a Complete-Information Static Game (CISG) to match energy among substations and absorb RBE/PV energy.
  2. Lower level: use a decoupled Q-learning algorithm to control each ESS and compensate the remaining traction-power demand.

The main design idea is:

use game theory for wide-area coordination, and reinforcement learning for local adaptive storage control.

1. Problem addressed

Traditional railway traction power systems are divided into feeding sections.

A train under regenerative braking may produce useful electrical energy, but this Regenerative Braking Energy (RBE) can be difficult to transfer to trains in another section.

A RIES adds:

  • Railway Power Conditioners (RPCs);
  • Energy Storage Systems (ESSs);
  • photovoltaic (PV) generation;
  • shared energy-transfer paths between traction substations.

This makes cross-regional energy sharing possible, but creates a new control problem:

how can several substations coordinate energy transfer in real time without creating an extremely large optimization problem?

2. Why existing methods are not enough

Rule-based control

Simple and fast, but thresholds depend heavily on engineering experience.

It is difficult to design rules for many interacting substations and changing train operations.

Model Predictive Control (MPC) and optimization

They can plan energy flow systematically, but performance depends on forecasts and models.

Irregular train schedules and a growing number of substations increase the optimization burden.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)

Methods such as DDPG, TD3, SAC, and DQN can handle complex problems.

However, the authors emphasize two concerns:

  • training and decision-making can become more complicated;
  • neural-network policies are less interpretable for railway engineering applications.

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)

MARL is attractive for multiple substations, but a large joint action space and non-stationary multi-agent interactions can make convergence difficult.

The paper therefore avoids using one method for the whole system.

3. RIES and two operating modes

Each traction substation contains:

  • two-phase traction loads;
  • an RPC;
  • an ESS;
  • a PV system.

The system can operate in two modes.

Independent operation

A substation mainly uses its own ESS and PV to manage local energy.

No wide-area energy exchange is required.

Cooperative operation

Several substations exchange energy through connected feeding sections.

The EMS must decide:

  • which substations should cooperate;
  • how much energy should be transferred;
  • when the system should remain locally independent.

This switching between local and cooperative operation is one of the key management problems.

4. Hierarchical Game-RL architecture

The EMS separates the problem into two levels.

Upper level — Primary Energy Flow Absorption (PEFA)

Main tasks:

  • match RBE with traction demand;
  • transfer energy between adjacent feeding sections;
  • absorb as much PV power as possible;
  • reduce energy exchange with the external grid.

The main actuator is the RPC network.

Lower level — Secondary Energy Flow Compensation (SEFC)

After the upper level finishes energy matching, some power imbalance remains.

The ESS then:

  • absorbs remaining regenerative energy;
  • absorbs unused PV energy;
  • supplies traction demand;
  • reduces traction power peaks.

This decomposition reduces the size of the decision problem at each level.

5. Why game theory is used at the upper level

Cross-regional RBE utilization is essentially an energy matching problem among several substations.

Each feeding section can be treated as a player.

The player’s strategy is the discretized RPC power-transfer decision.

The paper uses a Complete-Information Static Game (CISG):

  • every player knows the relevant system states;
  • all players select strategies under complete information;
  • the action space is finite and discretized.

The payoff encourages:

  • lower total traction-energy exchange;
  • energy transfer toward sections that need more compensation.

The authors use a dominant-strategy equilibrium to obtain consistent decisions among neighboring feeding sections.

A key claimed advantage is that the final matching does not require a large iterative numerical optimization at every control step.

6. Primary energy flow absorption

The upper level has two main objectives.

RBE utilization

Regenerative energy should be transferred to trains that are currently consuming traction power.

Priority is given to:

  • the same feeding section;
  • adjacent feeding sections;
  • nearby traction substations when cross-regional transfer is possible.

PV utilization

PV generation is first used locally.

If local demand is insufficient, remaining PV energy can support neighboring sections or charge the ESS.

So PEFA tries to reduce two forms of waste:

unused regenerative braking energy + curtailed PV energy

7. Why Q-learning is used for the lower ESS control

After primary energy matching, the ESS must react to rapidly changing two-phase traction loads.

The authors choose tabular Q-learning instead of a neural-network policy because they want:

  • fast online table lookup;
  • transparent state-action relationships;
  • easier interpretation;
  • no online nonlinear optimization.

However, ordinary Q-learning still faces a problem:

the two-phase ESS action space grows rapidly when power is finely discretized.

This motivates the proposed decoupling method.

8. Decoupled Q-learning: the key RL idea

The algorithm divides ESS operation into two modes.

Independent compensation

When the two phases are in different operating conditions, only the phase that needs compensation is considered.

The state and action dimensions can therefore be reduced.

Simultaneous compensation

When both phases need similar compensation, their power demands are combined.

The ESS first decides the total compensation power, then allocates it between the two phases according to demand.

Instead of one very large Q-table, the method uses two smaller Q-tables:

  • \(Q_{in}\): independent compensation;
  • \(Q_{si}\): simultaneous compensation.

The main purpose is:

reduce dimensionality without replacing the transparent Q-table with a black-box neural network.

9. Distance-based sampling policy

Offline Q-learning still requires exploration.

Uniform random action sampling wastes many training samples on actions that are very far from the actual traction-power demand.

The paper therefore defines a distance between:

  • candidate ESS compensation power;
  • current power demand.

Actions closer to the actual demand receive higher sampling probability.

The idea is simple:

more realistic actions are explored more often.

The authors describe this as an experience-driven pre-training mechanism.

It improves learning efficiency while keeping the Q-learning structure unchanged.

10. Reward-function design

The RL reward combines several requirements.

It encourages the ESS to:

  • keep State of Charge (SoC) within limits;
  • choose the correct charging/discharging direction;
  • compensate the total substation energy demand;
  • compensate individual feeding-section demand;
  • preserve energy for high-power peak compensation.

This last point is important.

If the ESS spends too much energy during low-demand periods, it may have insufficient energy later when a large traction peak occurs.

So the reward balances:

frequent energy compensation ↔︎ preserving capacity for peak shaving

These two objectives are partly conflicting.

11. Data and real-time validation

The case study uses measured Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) traction-power data from three adjacent substations on the Baotou–Shenmu Railway in Inner Mongolia, China.

Training and testing use different measured daily datasets.

The control framework is also tested on a Real-Time Digital Simulator (RTDS) platform.

This gives the validation three useful features:

  • real railway power data;
  • separate training and test datasets;
  • real-time simulation of online EMS decisions.

12. RL convergence improvement

The distance-based sampling policy clearly improves offline training speed.

For the SS2 agent:

  • proposed distance-based sampling converges in 30,953 iterations;
  • traditional random sampling requires 51,568 iterations.

For SS3, after 6,000 iterations, the reward of the proposed method is:

20.27% higher

than with traditional random sampling.

So the main benefit of the sampling policy is not a new control objective.

It is:

faster learning of a useful Q-table.

13. Primary absorption results

The upper game-based PEFA transfers regenerative energy between feeding sections and coordinates PV utilization.

For SS2, the reported RBE utilization efficiencies of the two phases are approximately:

  • 89.97%
  • 89.89%

Across the whole RIES after the primary stage:

  • traction energy decreases from 334.04 MWh to 290.56 MWh;
  • 21.66 MWh of RBE is absorbed;
  • RBE absorption ratio: 75.01%;
  • overall PV utilization: 83.84%.

The remaining renewable/regenerative energy is then handled by the secondary ESS stage.

14. Secondary compensation and comparison methods

The decoupled Q-learning method is compared with:

  • Fixed Threshold Control (FTC);
  • Fuzzy Logic Control (FLC);
  • Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO);
  • a multi-timescale Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) method.

The paper reports that the proposed Q-learning method achieves energy-performance results close to PSO, but with much lower online decision time.

Reported single-decision time:

  • decoupled Q-learning: 0.54 ms
  • PSO: 2882.65 ms
  • multi-timescale DRL: 145.83 ms

This is one of the paper’s strongest practical results.

The proposed method sacrifices some modeling sophistication in exchange for extremely fast online lookup.

15. Overall energy-saving results

After combining the primary game-based stage and the secondary RL stage:

  • total RBE utilized: 28.39 MWh;
  • RBE utilization ratio: 98.31%;
  • PV utilization ratio: 96.75%;
  • total traction energy decreases from 334.04 MWh to 279.58 MWh;
  • traction-energy reduction: 16.31%.

So the hierarchical strategy uses the two stages for different purposes:

game → move available energy to where it is needed

RL → use ESS to absorb and compensate what remains

16. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Hierarchical control is useful when one method is not suitable for every subproblem

The paper does not force game theory or RL to solve the entire RIES.

Each method is assigned to the task where it is more suitable.

Lesson 2 — Wide-area coordination and local device control are different problems

Cross-regional energy matching requires coordination among several substations.

ESS control is mainly a local sequential decision problem.

Separating them reduces complexity.

Lesson 3 — Interpretability can influence algorithm choice

A more complex Deep RL method is not automatically preferable.

For safety-critical engineering applications, a transparent Q-table and very fast online lookup may be valuable.

Lesson 4 — Reducing the decision space can be as important as designing a more powerful algorithm

The decoupling idea improves Q-learning mainly by restructuring the state-action space.

This is a useful general lesson for large control problems.

17. Limitations

Important limitations and open issues include:

  • the upper-level game assumes complete information among substations;
  • RPC power decisions are discretized;
  • the main optimization focuses on active power;
  • reactive-power compensation and voltage control are not jointly optimized in the proposed EMS;
  • the Q-learning stage relies on offline training data;
  • economic cost and equipment degradation are not the main objectives;
  • large-scale ESS economic feasibility is not evaluated in detail;
  • validation uses RTDS rather than long-term railway field deployment.

The authors identify three future directions:

  • use game theory to guide Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL);
  • study wide-area compensation with larger ESSs and evaluate its economics;
  • use residual RPC capacity for reactive-power compensation, voltage support, and power-quality improvement.

18. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • game-guided MARL for many traction substations;
  • adaptive or online Q-table updating when train patterns change;
  • battery degradation-aware ESS control;
  • economic optimization including electricity price and demand charges;
  • stochastic or robust control under uncertain train schedules and PV generation;
  • active + reactive power hierarchical control;
  • joint energy efficiency and voltage/power-quality management;
  • safety-constrained RL for ESS operation;
  • comparison with MPC under the same measured railway dataset;
  • transfer learning between railway lines or different substation configurations;
  • sizing + operation co-optimization for RPC, PV, and ESS.

注记[P012] Al Taha et al. (2025) — An Efficient Method for Quantifying the Aggregate Flexibility of Plug-In Electric Vehicle Populations

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 16(4), 3142–3154, 2025
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2024.3384871
Main topics: Electric Vehicle (EV) aggregation; Distributed Energy Resource (DER); flexibility modeling; Minkowski sum; inner approximation; affine transformation; Linear Programming (LP); disaggregation

Quick takeaway

This paper studies a fundamental problem for large-scale EV aggregation:

How can an aggregator describe the combined charging flexibility of many heterogeneous EVs using one compact model that is safe, computationally tractable, and compatible with electricity-market rules?

The exact aggregate flexibility set is a Minkowski sum of all individual EV flexibility sets, but computing this set exactly is generally intractable.

The paper therefore develops efficient inner approximations based on affine transformations and linear programming.

The main goal is not to find the charging schedule itself, but to build a reliable aggregate flexibility model that can later be used by an optimizer or electricity market.

1. Problem addressed

An EV aggregator may control hundreds or thousands of EVs.

Each EV has different:

  • arrival and departure times;
  • charging/discharging power limits;
  • battery capacity;
  • initial State of Charge (SoC);
  • required final energy.

The aggregator needs to answer:

What total charging-power profiles can this whole EV population actually provide?

This is important because the aggregator may want to participate in:

  • energy markets;
  • ancillary-service markets;
  • peak-load management;
  • demand response.

However, the market normally does not want thousands of individual EV models.

It needs one aggregate resource model.

2. Individual EV flexibility: a generalized battery

For each EV \(i\), the charging profile is:

\[ u_i = [u_i(0),\ldots,u_i(T-1)] \]

The delivered energy evolves as:

\[ x_i(t+1)=x_i(t)+u_i(t)\delta \]

The feasible set of charging profiles is defined by:

  • minimum and maximum charging power;
  • minimum and maximum accumulated energy.

The paper writes this compactly as:

\[ \mathcal U_i=\{u\mid Hu\le h_i\} \]

This is a convex polytope in Half-space representation (H-representation).

A useful interpretation is:

each EV behaves like a small virtual battery with its own power and energy limits.

3. What is aggregate flexibility?

If there are \(N\) EVs, the total charging power is:

\[ u=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_i \]

The aggregate flexibility set is therefore:

\[ \mathcal U=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal U_i \]

where \(\oplus\) denotes the Minkowski sum.

In simple terms:

take every feasible charging profile of EV 1, add it to every feasible profile of EV 2, and continue for all EVs.

The final set contains every total charging profile that can be achieved without violating any individual EV constraint.

4. Why exact aggregation is difficult

The difficulty is computational.

Each EV flexibility set is naturally given as an H-polytope.

But computing the exact Minkowski sum of H-polytopes is generally hard, and converting between:

  • H-representation: constraints;
  • Vertex representation (V-representation): corner points;

can grow exponentially with problem dimension.

So exact aggregation becomes impractical when:

  • the time horizon is long;
  • the number of EVs is large;
  • EV parameters are heterogeneous.

This is why the paper does approximation rather than exact aggregation.

5. Why an inner approximation is preferred

There are two basic approximation types.

Outer approximation

It contains the true flexibility set.

Advantage:

  • it may look less conservative.

Problem:

  • it can contain charging profiles that the EV population cannot actually deliver.

Inner approximation

It is fully contained inside the true flexibility set.

Advantage:

  • every aggregate profile inside it is guaranteed feasible.

For control and market participation, this safety property is important.

The paper therefore focuses on:

\[ \mathcal P \subseteq \mathcal U \]

where \(\mathcal P\) is a tractable inner approximation.

6. Core idea: transform one base flexibility set

The paper first defines a common base set \(\mathcal U_0\).

It is built from the average parameters of the individual EV flexibility sets.

Then, instead of describing each EV with a completely new geometry, the paper approximates each EV using an affine transformation of this base set:

\[ \gamma_i+\Gamma_i\mathcal U_0 \]

where:

  • \(\gamma_i\): translation;
  • \(\Gamma_i\): linear transformation.

The transformed set must remain inside the true individual flexibility set:

\[ \gamma_i+\Gamma_i\mathcal U_0 \subseteq \mathcal U_i \]

The individual approximations are then combined to construct one safe aggregate approximation.

The important idea is:

approximate locally → combine safely → obtain one aggregate model.

7. Why affine transformation is better than simple scaling

Earlier methods often use a homothetic transformation:

\[ \gamma_i+\alpha_i\mathcal U_0 \]

This only allows:

  • translation;
  • uniform scaling.

That works well when EV flexibility sets have similar shapes.

But heterogeneous EVs can have very different:

  • charging windows;
  • power limits;
  • energy requirements.

Simple scaling may therefore waste a large amount of usable flexibility.

The proposed affine transformation:

\[ \gamma_i+\Gamma_i\mathcal U_0 \]

can stretch, shrink, and reshape the base set in different directions.

This provides more freedom and usually a less conservative approximation.

8. Two approximation models

The paper develops two main versions.

Structure-preserving approximation

The final aggregate approximation must still look like a generalized battery.

It uses:

\[ \mathcal P=p+\alpha\mathcal U_0 \]

The main advantage is:

the result can be represented using familiar aggregate power and energy limits.

This is important for electricity-market compatibility.

General affine approximation

The final approximation is:

\[ \mathcal P=p+P\mathcal U_0 \]

where \(P\) is a general matrix.

This gives more geometric freedom and usually improves approximation accuracy.

However, the resulting set may no longer have a simple battery-style representation.

So there is a clear trade-off:

market-friendly representation ↔︎ approximation accuracy

9. How the difficult set-containment problem becomes an LP

A key mathematical contribution is converting the condition:

\[ \gamma_i+\Gamma_i\mathcal U_0\subseteq\mathcal U_i \]

into linear constraints using a polytope-containment result related to Farkas’ Lemma and Linear Programming (LP) duality.

This means the original difficult geometric problem can be conservatively handled with LPs.

The final optimization size grows polynomially with:

  • number of EVs \(N\);
  • time dimension \(T\).

This is the main reason the method can scale better than exact Minkowski-sum calculations.

10. Structure-preserving versus general affine LP

For the structure-preserving case, the LP mainly maximizes the scaling factor:

\[ \max \alpha \]

A larger \(\alpha\) means a larger safe aggregate flexibility region.

For the general affine case, directly maximizing volume is still difficult.

The authors therefore approximate the volume objective using:

\[ \max \operatorname{Tr}(P) \]

This creates another LP.

The general affine formulation also has an important computational advantage:

the individual EV approximation problems can be solved independently.

So the computation can be parallelized.

11. Disaggregation: from aggregate command back to individual EVs

Aggregation alone is not enough.

Suppose the market sends the aggregator one feasible total charging profile:

\[ u \]

The aggregator must convert this into:

\[ u_1,u_2,\ldots,u_N \]

so that every EV remains feasible.

A common solution is to solve another optimization problem.

This paper avoids that.

The affine mappings obtained during aggregation can be reused directly.

For an aggregate point inside the proposed inner approximation, the individual charging profiles can be recovered by:

\[ u_i=\gamma_i+\Gamma_iP^{-1}(u-p) \]

when \(P\) is invertible.

So:

aggregate schedule → direct affine mapping → individual EV schedules

No additional disaggregation optimization is required.

This is an important practical advantage.

12. Case study: what is being tested?

The paper tests whether the approximate aggregate model is good enough for real downstream optimization tasks.

Two applications are considered.

Peak-power minimization

The EV population is controlled to reduce the maximum household net load.

Electricity-cost minimization

The EVs charge when electricity is cheap and can discharge when prices are high.

The study uses:

  • household load data from Pecan Street Dataport;
  • New York Independent System Operator (NYISO) day-ahead electricity prices;
  • 25 simulated EVs;
  • 18 one-hour time periods;
  • six months of daily data.

Four approximation methods are compared:

  1. proposed general affine;
  2. proposed structure-preserving;
  3. homothet-based method;
  4. zonotope-based method.

The true aggregate flexibility set is also used as the benchmark.

13. Main findings: approximation quality

For peak-power minimization:

  • the general affine method performs best;
  • its suboptimality gap is almost zero for more than half of the tested days.

For electricity-cost minimization:

  • the general affine and structure-preserving methods generally outperform the homothet and zonotope approaches.

The zonotope method also shows larger day-to-day variation.

The key conclusion is:

a more accurate flexibility geometry leads to better downstream control and scheduling decisions.

14. Accuracy versus usability

The paper does not claim that one method is best for every purpose.

General affine

Best when:

  • approximation accuracy is the priority;
  • a general polytope representation is acceptable;
  • parallel computation is available.

Structure-preserving

Best when:

  • the result must look like one energy-storage resource;
  • compatibility with market bid formats is important;
  • slightly more conservatism is acceptable.

This distinction is practically important.

A mathematically tighter model is not always the most useful market model.

15. Computational scalability

The paper also tests EV populations from:

20 to 200 EVs

The reported trend is:

  • structure-preserving computation grows faster than linearly;
  • general affine computation grows approximately linearly.

The reason is that the general affine problem can be decomposed into one LP per EV.

These LPs can also be solved in parallel.

So the general affine approach has a strong scalability advantage.

The authors note that the structure-preserving LP also has block structure that may be exploited using decomposition methods such as Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition.

16. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Flexibility itself must be modeled before it can be optimized

Before asking:

“What is the best EV charging schedule?”

we first need to know:

“What charging schedules are actually possible for the whole EV population?”

Lesson 2 — Aggregation is a model-reduction problem

Thousands of flexible devices must be represented by one manageable model.

The challenge is to reduce complexity without removing too much usable flexibility.

Lesson 3 — Safe approximation matters

An inner approximation may be conservative, but every schedule inside it is implementable.

This is valuable for market bidding and real-time control.

Lesson 4 — Aggregation and disaggregation should be designed together

It is not enough to build one aggregate model.

The aggregator must also be able to translate a market-level command back to feasible individual-device commands.

17. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • the main model assumes lossless EV charging dynamics;
  • general charging/discharging efficiency losses can make individual flexibility sets nonconvex;
  • the case study assumes perfect knowledge of household load and electricity prices;
  • the aggregator is assumed to be a price taker;
  • distribution-network constraints are not included;
  • the structure-preserving approximation is still visibly conservative;
  • experiments mainly use dozens to hundreds of EVs rather than very large field fleets.

The authors specifically identify:

  • general lossy charging models;
  • distribution-network capacity constraints;
  • hierarchical aggregation

as future directions.

18. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • hierarchical aggregation for thousands or millions of EVs;
  • network-constrained EV flexibility aggregation;
  • stochastic flexibility under uncertain arrival/departure times;
  • data-driven aggregate flexibility models;
  • nonconvex flexibility caused by efficiency losses or discrete charging states;
  • dynamic aggregate models that update online as EVs connect and disconnect;
  • aggregation including battery degradation;
  • EV flexibility + electricity-market bidding;
  • flexibility aggregation for EVs + HVAC + batteries together;
  • robust aggregation under uncertain user behavior;
  • comparison between geometric, optimization-based, and learning-based flexibility models.

注记[P013] Chen et al. (2023) — An Energy Sharing Mechanism Considering Network Constraints and Market Power Limitation

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(2), 1027–1041, 2023
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2022.3198721
Main topics: energy sharing; prosumer market; Generalized Nash Equilibrium (GNE); network constraints; market power; price regulation; Locational Marginal Price (LMP); distributed bidding

Quick takeaway

This paper studies a difficult problem in prosumer energy sharing:

How can prosumers actively bid and trade energy through a network, while preventing congestion and market-power manipulation?

The authors first design a network-aware energy sharing market in which prosumers are price-makers and can become buyers or sellers endogenously.

However, they show that congestion can create serious market problems:

  • no equilibrium;
  • multiple equilibria;
  • inefficient equilibria.

They then add a price regulation policy that limits each prosumer’s ability to manipulate the trading price.

The improved mechanism guarantees a unique, socially near-optimal equilibrium and can be implemented through an iterative bidding algorithm.

1. Problem addressed

Traditional centralized energy management becomes less attractive when many prosumers own Distributed Energy Resources (DERs).

Important issues include:

  • different prosumers have conflicting economic interests;
  • private cost information should not be fully revealed;
  • low feed-in tariffs may give weak incentives for renewable-energy sharing;
  • network congestion limits who can trade with whom;
  • strategic prosumers may deliberately change bids to influence prices.

The paper therefore asks:

Can an energy-sharing market preserve privacy and flexibility, satisfy network limits, and still avoid market-power abuse?

2. Self-sufficiency versus energy sharing

Each prosumer is asked to reduce its purchase from the main grid by a target amount \(D_i\).

Under self-sufficiency, prosumer \(i\) must provide the entire reduction by itself:

\[ p_i=D_i \]

where \(p_i\) is its net production adjustment.

This can be inefficient.

If Prosumer A has a low adjustment cost and Prosumer B has a high adjustment cost, it may be cheaper for A to adjust more and sell energy to B.

Energy sharing therefore allows:

\[ p_i+q_i=D_i \]

where:

  • \(q_i>0\): prosumer \(i\) buys energy;
  • \(q_i<0\): prosumer \(i\) sells energy.

The system still achieves the required total reduction, but individual prosumers can share the adjustment burden.

3. Prosumers are price-makers, not passive price-takers

The paper uses the generalized bidding relation:

\[ q_i=-a\lambda_i+b_i \]

where:

  • \(q_i\): sharing quantity;
  • \(\lambda_i\): local sharing price;
  • \(b_i\): prosumer bid;
  • \(a\): market sensitivity parameter.

A prosumer chooses \(b_i\), knowing that its bid can influence the market-clearing price.

This makes the prosumer a price-maker.

Another important feature is that the buyer/seller role is not fixed beforehand.

A prosumer becomes a buyer or seller depending on its bid relative to the other bids.

So market roles are endogenous.

4. Market clearing includes network constraints

The platform clears the market subject to:

  • total energy balance;
  • transmission/distribution line-flow limits.

A Direct Current (DC) power-flow model is used.

The network effect of each prosumer is represented through line-flow distribution factors.

The platform solves a price-clearing problem that minimizes:

\[ \sum_i \lambda_i^2 \]

This is equivalent to reducing the variance of prices across prosumers.

So the platform is not designed to maximize its own profit.

Its purpose is to:

clear the market while reducing unnecessary price discrimination.

When there is no congestion, all prosumers receive the same sharing price.

When congestion occurs, nodal prices can differ.

5. Basic energy-sharing process

The basic process is:

  1. each prosumer submits a bid \(b_i\);
  2. the platform calculates network-feasible prices \(\lambda_i\);
  3. sharing quantities \(q_i\) are determined;
  4. each prosumer chooses its own production adjustment;
  5. prosumers update their bids.

Because each player’s feasible decision depends on the other players through market-clearing and network constraints, the market is modeled as a Generalized Nash Game (GNG).

Its equilibrium is a Generalized Nash Equilibrium (GNE).

6. Why network congestion creates a market-power problem

Without strong congestion, the market can behave well.

In a large market, each prosumer has little influence on price, and the equilibrium approaches the centralized social optimum.

The problem appears when:

  • the market is relatively small;
  • a transmission line becomes congested;
  • one participant can strongly influence its local price.

A strategic prosumer may then keep changing its bid while the physical line flow is already fixed at its limit.

This can create:

  • market failure: no GNE;
  • market instability: multiple GNEs;
  • market inefficiency: an equilibrium far from the desired social outcome.

This is why network-aware market design cannot stop at simply adding line-flow constraints.

7. Counterexample: congestion can produce multiple equilibria

The paper uses simple two- and three-prosumer examples to demonstrate the problem.

In the two-prosumer case:

  • when the line is not congested, the game has a unique equilibrium;
  • when the line becomes congested, a range of equilibria may appear.

The physical power flow may be fixed by the line limit, but strategic bids can still change prices.

This creates room for market-power exploitation.

The three-prosumer example shows an even more serious result:

depending on the parameters, the original market may have no GNE, one GNE, or infinitely many GNEs.

So equilibrium existence and uniqueness cannot be taken for granted.

8. Price regulation: the main solution

To solve the market-power problem, the paper introduces a price regulation policy.

The basic idea is:

A prosumer should not be allowed to push its trading price arbitrarily far away from its marginal production-adjustment cost.

The regulation limits the prosumer’s price privilege according to:

  • its sharing quantity \(|q_i|\);
  • market sensitivity \(a\);
  • number of prosumers \(I\).

The maximum price privilege is proportional to:

\[ \frac{|q_i|}{a(I-1)} \]

This means market power becomes weaker when:

  • the market contains more prosumers;
  • market sensitivity is larger;
  • the prosumer’s trading quantity is small.

9. What price regulation achieves

With price regulation, the improved mechanism guarantees several important properties.

Unique equilibrium

The improved game has a unique GNE.

So the no-equilibrium and multiple-equilibrium problems are removed.

Individual rationality

Every prosumer’s cost under energy sharing is no higher than under self-sufficiency.

Therefore, no participant becomes worse off by joining.

This gives a clear economic incentive to participate.

Near-social optimum

The equilibrium is not always exactly equal to the centralized optimum.

However, the efficiency loss becomes smaller as the number of prosumers increases.

Market power is limited

A single prosumer cannot obtain an unlimited price advantage through strategic bidding.

10. Price of Anarchy and market size

The paper uses Price of Anarchy (PoA) to measure the efficiency loss caused by strategic behavior.

\[ \text{PoA} = \frac{\text{cost at equilibrium}} {\text{cost at social optimum}} \]

A value of:

\[ \text{PoA}=1 \]

means the market equilibrium is socially optimal.

The paper proves that:

\[ \text{PoA}\rightarrow1 \quad\text{as}\quad I\rightarrow\infty \]

So the improved sharing market becomes more efficient as the number of prosumers grows.

This gives an intuitive result:

market power becomes weaker in a larger market.

11. Energy-sharing prices have an LMP-like structure

At equilibrium, the regulated sharing price can be decomposed into:

  • a system-wide energy-balance component;
  • line-congestion components.

This is structurally similar to a Locational Marginal Price (LMP).

So the local sharing price reflects where the prosumer is located in the network.

If there is no congestion:

all prosumers obtain the same price.

If lines are congested:

prices become location-dependent.

The mechanism therefore connects local energy sharing with familiar electricity-market pricing principles.

12. Bidding algorithm

The paper develops a practical iterative bidding process.

Each iteration contains two steps.

Platform update

Given current bids, the platform updates sharing prices while:

  • satisfying network constraints;
  • balancing energy;
  • avoiding large price oscillations.

Prosumer update

Each prosumer updates:

  • its production adjustment;
  • its bid.

The process repeats until the bids stop changing significantly.

This allows the equilibrium to be reached without asking every prosumer to reveal its complete cost function to the platform.

13. Why the market sensitivity parameter \(a\) matters

The parameter \(a\) controls how strongly sharing quantity reacts to price.

If \(a\) is too small:

  • a small price change causes a strong bid response;
  • bids can oscillate;
  • the algorithm may diverge.

If \(a\) is larger:

  • the bidding process becomes more stable;
  • but convergence can be slower.

The paper provides a sufficient lower bound on \(a\) that guarantees convergence.

The authors explain this using the economic cobweb model.

So \(a\) is not only a market-demand parameter.

It is also an algorithm-stability parameter.

14. Effect of price regulation in the simple case

The two-prosumer example clearly illustrates the main mechanism.

When the line limit is loose:

  • one unique GNE exists.

When the line is congested and there is no regulation:

  • multiple GNEs appear.

After price regulation:

注记[P014] Rouhani et al. (2024) — Barrier-Function Adaptive Finite-Time Trajectory Tracking Controls for Cyber Resilience in Smart Grids Under an Electricity Market Environment

DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2024.3417704
Main topics: cyber resilience; real-time electricity market; Automatic Generation Control (AGC); False Data Injection Attack (FDIA); Sliding Mode Control (SMC); finite-time tracking; barrier function; communication delay

Quick takeaway

This paper studies a cyber-resilience problem at the interface between the real-time electricity market and power-system frequency control.

The key question is:

When market contracts between Generation Companies (GENCOs) and Distribution Companies (DISCOs) change in real time, how can the power system maintain frequency stability if false data are injected into the control and measurement channels at the same time?

The authors propose a barrier-function adaptive finite-time second-order sliding-mode controller.

Its main goals are to:

  • reject False Data Injection Attacks (FDIAs);
  • handle load variations and model uncertainty;
  • tolerate communication delay and governor dead-band nonlinearity;
  • avoid control chattering;
  • drive tracking errors into a small region in finite time.

1. Problem addressed

In a deregulated electricity market, DISCOs can sign contracts with GENCOs in different control areas.

These contracts can change with market prices.

Changing contracts alter:

  • scheduled generation;
  • tie-line power;
  • Area Control Error (ACE);
  • generator reference signals.

So even under normal conditions, real-time market operation creates frequent power-flow and frequency changes.

This makes cyber defense harder because the controller must distinguish between:

  • legitimate market-driven changes;
  • load disturbances;
  • system uncertainty;
  • malicious false data.

The paper focuses on keeping the system stable even when these effects occur together.

2. Why the real-time bidding market creates a cyber vulnerability

The paper models contracts using a DISCO Participation Matrix (DPM).

Each element \(cpf_{ij}\) represents the contract between GENCO \(i\) and DISCO \(j\).

When electricity prices change, the \(cpf_{ij}\) values change.

This directly modifies the power commands used by AGC.

The difficulty is that an attacker can inject false data during the same period in which contracts are changing.

Therefore:

normal market fluctuation and malicious data can look similar to the controller.

This creates a useful attack window.

3. Control objective

The system output includes variables such as:

  • frequency deviation;
  • tie-line power deviation;
  • ACE.

The controller should force the measured output \(y(t)\) to track a desired reference \(r(t)\).

The tracking error is:

\[ e(t)=y(t)-r(t) \]

The control target is not only asymptotic stability.

The authors want the error to enter the desired region within finite time.

This is important because cyber-attack rejection in AGC is a real-time task.

4. How FDIA is represented

Cyber-attacks are modeled as unknown disturbances acting on system states and outputs.

For numerical testing, the paper uses a random scaling attack:

\[ y(t)=(1+\zeta(t))y(t) \]

during the attack period, with:

\[ \zeta(t)\in[-0.5,0.5] \]

So the attacker can distort measurements by up to roughly 50% in either direction in the test setup.

The simulations also include:

  • 10% system-parameter variation;
  • load disturbances;
  • communication time delay;
  • governor dead-band nonlinearity.

5. Moving attacked outputs into an augmented state model

The paper first separates system outputs into:

  • outputs without direct attack;
  • outputs that may contain attack signals and direct market inputs.

An orthogonal decomposition matrix and a stable filter are then used.

The filter moves the attacked-output effect into an augmented state-space model.

This creates a new system representation in which:

  • market/load variations appear mainly as matched disturbances;
  • cyber-attack effects can appear as unmatched disturbances.

This reformulation makes the following sliding-mode controller easier to design.

6. Why conventional sliding-mode control is not enough

Sliding Mode Control (SMC) is attractive because it is robust to uncertainty and disturbances.

However, the paper highlights two limitations.

Chattering

Conventional switching SMC can produce high-frequency oscillations in the control signal.

This is undesirable for practical power-system control.

Slow asymptotic convergence

Standard asymptotic stability only guarantees that the error approaches zero as time goes to infinity.

For real-time cyber defense, the controller should recover much faster.

The proposed method therefore combines:

  • second-order sliding mode;
  • finite-time convergence;
  • adaptive gain;
  • barrier-function regulation.

7. PID-type second-order sliding surface

The paper defines a PID sliding surface:

\[ s_e(t) = k_p e(t) + k_i\int_0^t e(\tau)d\tau + k_d\dot e(t) \]

The second-order design aims to drive both:

\[ s_e(t)\rightarrow0 \]

and

\[ \dot{s}_e(t)\rightarrow0 \]

in finite time.

If the gains are selected properly, the resulting tracking-error dynamics are stable.

Using a second-order sliding surface also helps reduce chattering compared with a conventional first-order switching controller.

8. Proposed finite-time control law

The controller contains two main components.

Equivalent control

This part compensates the nominal system dynamics.

Switching / robust control

This part rejects:

  • uncertainty;
  • disturbances;
  • cyber-attack effects.

The discontinuous term is applied to the derivative of the control input rather than directly to the control input.

After integration, the actual control signal becomes smoother.

This is the main reason the authors describe the proposed controller as chattering-free.

9. Adaptive barrier-function law

A major challenge is that the upper bounds of disturbances and attack effects are not known in practice.

A fixed robust gain would therefore be difficult to select.

The paper uses a two-stage adaptive mechanism.

Stage 1 — Adaptive gain

Before the tracking error reaches a small neighborhood of the sliding surface, the control gain increases according to the sliding error.

This helps the controller reach the desired region quickly.

Stage 2 — Barrier function

After the error enters the prescribed region, a positive-semidefinite barrier function becomes active.

Its purpose is to keep the error inside the region and reduce unnecessary overestimation of the control gain.

So the controller tries to achieve:

fast approach + small steady error + no excessive control gain

10. Finite-time stability guarantee

The paper provides Lyapunov-based proofs for two operating stages:

  • before the tracking error reaches the prescribed vicinity;
  • after the trajectory enters that vicinity.

The main theoretical result is that the sliding variables reach the required region in finite time under the stated assumptions.

This is stronger than simply showing asymptotic convergence.

For this paper, finite-time convergence is important because the controller is intended to reject cyber-attacks online.

11. Overall computational procedure

The complete procedure can be summarized as:

  1. build the multi-area deregulated power-system model;
  2. include market contract changes and cyber-attack channels;
  3. decompose attacked and non-attacked outputs;
  4. construct the augmented filtered state-space model;
  5. define tracking error;
  6. design the PID second-order sliding surface;
  7. apply adaptive finite-time control;
  8. switch to barrier-function gain regulation after entering the desired region;
  9. verify frequency stability under attack.

12. Case study

The main numerical case uses an IEEE 39-bus system divided into three control areas.

Some generators provide spinning reserve and participate in AGC.

The market side is represented by changing GENCO–DISCO contracts.

The study includes:

  • online contract changes;
  • FDIA during the contract-changing periods;
  • communication delay;
  • governor dead-band nonlinearity;
  • 10% parameter uncertainty.

The proposed controller is compared with Active Disturbance Rejection Control (ADRC).

13. Main findings

When bidding contracts change and FDIA occurs, frequency deviations appear in all three control areas.

The proposed controller quickly drives these deviations back toward the operating region.

Better performance than ADRC

The comparison shows that the proposed method gives faster and more accurate frequency recovery under the tested attack conditions.

The authors attribute this to:

  • the filtered augmented-state formulation;
  • the second-order sliding surface;
  • finite-time tracking;
  • adaptive barrier-function gain.

Chattering mitigation

Because the discontinuous action is applied to the derivative of the controller rather than directly to the control signal, the final control input is smoother.

14. Real-time experimental validation

The method is also tested using:

  • a Speedgoat Real-Time Target Machine;
  • Simulink Real-Time;
  • an FPGA-based I/O platform.

The experimental results reproduce the main behavior found in simulation:

  • attack rejection;
  • frequency recovery;
  • fast sliding-surface convergence.

The authors also test an IEEE 118-bus system to show that the approach can be applied to a larger power-system model.

15. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Market dynamics and control security are coupled

Real-time market contracts are not only an economic issue.

They change physical power-flow and AGC reference signals.

This can affect how cyber-attacks appear to the controller.

Lesson 2 — Cyber resilience does not always require a separate attack detector

The proposed strategy focuses on robust rejection of the attack effect.

The controller is designed to keep the system stable even without first identifying the exact attack signal.

A controller that is theoretically stable but very slow may be unsuitable for online attack rejection.

A fixed very large robust gain may cause poor control quality.

The adaptive barrier-function mechanism tries to provide only the gain needed to keep the trajectory inside the desired region.

16. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • the numerical FDIA is mainly represented by a random scaling attack;
  • the method assumes disturbances are bounded;
  • several structural assumptions are required for the system decomposition and stability proof;
  • the attack is rejected, but the method does not identify the attacker or attack location;
  • the market model is used mainly to generate contract-driven dynamics rather than to study strategic bidding or market equilibrium;
  • controller tuning still requires several design parameters;
  • the main validation is simulation and real-time target-machine testing rather than long-term field deployment;
  • the approach does not explicitly optimize the economic cost of cyber-resilient control.

17. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • resilient Model Predictive Control (MPC) under real-time market cyber-attacks;
  • attack detection + attack rejection in one architecture;
  • event-triggered or communication-efficient cyber-resilient AGC;
  • adaptive protection against unknown or learning-based attacks;

  • secure AGC under renewable uncertainty and low inertia;

Quick takeaway

This paper studies a large-scale electric-bus operation problem:

How can an urban Electric Bus Operator (EBO) jointly schedule the charging of hundreds of buses and decide how much energy and reserve capacity to bid into electricity markets?

The difficulty is that:

  • each bus has several trips and several charging windows;
  • trip energy consumption is uncertain;
  • individual-bus optimization becomes very large;
  • an aggregated charging plan may be impossible to decompose back to individual buses.

The paper solves this by combining:

  • a trip-chain based flexibility boundary model;
  • a state-based aggregation method;
  • a hierarchical stochastic optimization framework;
  • accelerated Benders decomposition to preserve disaggregation feasibility.

1. Problem addressed

An urban EBO may manage:

  • many electric bus lines;
  • several Electric Bus Stations (EBSs);
  • hundreds of Electric Buses (EBs);
  • station-level Energy Storage Systems (ESSs).

The EBO must guarantee that every bus has enough energy for its scheduled trips.

At the same time, the fleet has substantial charging flexibility because a bus does not always need to recharge immediately after each trip.

This creates an economic opportunity:

  • buy electricity when prices are low;
  • avoid high-price periods;
  • use bus and ESS flexibility to provide reserve services.

The central problem is therefore:

how to convert operational flexibility of a large bus fleet into market value without making the optimization problem computationally intractable.

2. Why electric buses are different from ordinary EV charging

Ordinary fast-charging stations mainly react to arriving vehicles.

The charging station operator usually cannot control the travel schedule of those vehicles.

An EBO is different.

It typically knows and manages:

  • bus timetables;
  • trip chains;
  • station assignment;
  • charging windows.

So the EBO has much more scheduling freedom.

A bus may complete multiple trips in one day and recharge between some of them.

This means the key flexibility is not simply:

arrival → charge → departure

but:

trip → charging window → trip → charging window → …

3. Why existing single-trip boundary models are insufficient

Existing EV flexibility models often use upper/lower boundaries of:

  • charging power;
  • accumulated charged energy.

For one charging window, this works well.

However, a bus with several sequential trips has temporal coupling:

  • how much it charged after Trip 1 affects the available energy before Trip 2;
  • the charging decision in one window changes the feasible region of later windows.

So a single-trip boundary model cannot simply be repeated independently for every trip.

The paper therefore develops a trip-chain based boundary model.

4. Trip-chain based multi-trip flexibility model

The basic idea is to represent the charging flexibility of one bus by:

  • upper/lower charging-power bounds;
  • upper/lower accumulated-energy bounds.

The energy boundaries are updated across multiple trips using the accumulated trip-energy consumption.

The upper energy boundary represents:

the maximum energy that the bus can absorb while respecting battery and charging limits.

The lower boundary represents:

the minimum energy that must be charged to guarantee the next scheduled trip.

So the region between the two curves is the bus’s charging flexibility.

5. Why the boundary model is useful

Instead of optimizing every charging decision first, the boundary model tells the optimizer:

What charging trajectories are feasible?

This is a compact flexibility representation.

The optimizer can then choose any charging trajectory that stays inside:

  • power limits;
  • energy limits;
  • trip requirements.

This substantially reduces the number of variables needed in large-scale scheduling.

6. Trip-energy uncertainty makes the boundary uncertain

The actual energy consumed by a bus trip is uncertain because of:

  • traffic conditions;
  • weather;
  • passenger load;
  • driving conditions.

So the amount of energy required after a trip is not fixed.

The paper therefore builds uncertain energy boundaries.

If a trip consumes more energy than expected:

  • the upper energy boundary shifts;
  • the lower energy boundary also shifts.

The paper uses a scenario-based representation so that different trip-energy outcomes generate different feasible charging regions.

7. Why simple aggregation can become unsafe

A natural idea is:

sum the boundaries of all buses.

But for multi-trip buses, this can enlarge the feasible region.

The aggregate model may then contain a total charging trajectory that looks feasible at station level but cannot be decomposed into valid schedules for individual buses.

This is a critical issue:

aggregate feasibility does not automatically guarantee individual feasibility.

The paper solves this problem with a new state-based aggregation plus Benders decomposition.

8. State-based aggregation

Every bus is classified into one of four states.

S1 — Available

The bus is inside the EBS and can charge.

S2 — On-duty

The bus is away from the station and is operating on its route.

S3 — Just-left

The bus has just started a trip.

S4 — Return

The bus is about to return to the EBS.

The state transitions depend only on the known timetable.

This allows the EBS to aggregate energy boundaries separately for each state.

Only buses in S1 are available for charging.

The state-based model retains more temporal information than directly summing all individual boundaries.

9. Charger availability is also included

The total charging flexibility is not only limited by bus batteries.

It is also limited by the number of chargers in each EBS.

If many buses are parked but only a few chargers are available:

not all buses can charge simultaneously.

The paper therefore modifies the aggregate power and energy boundaries according to the available number of chargers.

This tightens the feasible region and avoids overly optimistic aggregate flexibility.

10. Hierarchical operation framework

The optimization is divided into two layers.

Upper layer — EBO

The EBO decides:

  • day-ahead electricity purchases;
  • balancing-market adjustments;
  • reserve-market bids;
  • aggregated station-level charging targets.

Lower layer — EBS

Each station determines:

  • individual EB charging schedules;
  • whether the aggregate power command can be decomposed feasibly.

The information exchange has two directions.

Bottom-up: EBSs report aggregate flexibility boundaries.

Top-down: the EBO sends aggregate charging and reserve decisions to the EBSs.

11. Electricity markets considered

The EBO participates in three markets.

Day-Ahead Market (DAM)

The EBO schedules electricity purchases before real operation.

The aim is to buy more electricity during cheaper periods.

Balancing Market (BM)

If actual energy consumption differs from the day-ahead plan because of trip uncertainty, the imbalance is settled in the balancing market.

Reserve Market (RM)

Unused charging flexibility of EBs and ESSs can be offered as reserve capacity.

This allows the EBO to earn additional revenue.

So the operation problem is not only:

minimize charging cost

but:

energy cost + balancing cost − reserve revenue + shortage penalties

12. Role of the station ESS

Each EBS contains an ESS.

The ESS provides several kinds of flexibility:

  • charge during low-price periods;
  • discharge during high-price periods;
  • buffer fast-charging peaks;
  • provide up/down reserve;
  • reduce dependence on the balancing market.

The EB fleet and ESS therefore work together.

The EBs provide large but schedule-dependent flexibility.

The ESS provides more directly controllable power and energy flexibility.

13. Why Benders decomposition is needed

The upper-level model wants to work with aggregate station variables.

But the true feasibility conditions belong to individual buses.

This naturally creates:

  • a master problem at the EBO level;
  • many slave problems at the EBS/scenario level.

The master problem decides the aggregated market and station schedules.

Each slave problem checks:

Can this aggregate solution actually be decomposed to individual EBs while respecting trip and reserve constraints?

If not, the slave problem generates a cut and sends it back to the master problem.

The process repeats until the aggregate plan is individually feasible.

14. Accelerated Benders decomposition

Basic Benders decomposition can still be slow.

The paper accelerates it using:

  • multiple cuts;
  • parallel computation;
  • modified Pareto-optimal cuts.

This improves convergence while maintaining the hierarchical structure.

The key practical advantage is:

the EBO does not need to solve all individual EB variables inside one huge centralized model.

The EBO purchases more electricity during low-price periods and avoids charging during expensive periods.

15:00–18:00

Buses tend to recharge earlier when electricity is cheaper.

The paper compares cases with different ESS and transformer capacities.

battery energy capacity + battery power capacity + transformer capacity

rather than the ESS alone.

Lower level

Encourages direct trading among users.

A low-emission user can monetize its surplus CERs.

This transfers part of the emission cost from cleaner users to higher-emission users.

Upper level

Acts as a regulatory backstop.

If a buyer cannot obtain enough CERs from other MELAs, it must purchase additional rights from the emission-control sector.

The upper-level price therefore prevents a high-emission user from simply ignoring its carbon responsibility.

The resulting logic is:

trade first with other users if attractive → buy remaining CERs from the regulator if necessary.

5. Demand-side carbon emissions are calculated from energy-carbon flow

A MELA’s carbon emission is not determined only by its total energy demand.

It also depends on the carbon intensity of the energy supplied to its node.

The paper uses a carbon-emission flow model.

For electricity and gas networks, node carbon intensity reflects the weighted average carbon embedded in incoming energy flows.

The MELA’s emission is then approximately:

\[ E^D_{t,j} = \rho^e_{t,j}P^D_{t,j} + \rho^g_{t,j}Q^D_{t,j} \]

where:

  • \(P^D\): electricity demand;
  • \(Q^D\): gas demand;
  • \(\rho^e\): electricity carbon intensity;
  • \(\rho^g\): gas carbon intensity.

So the same amount of electricity can cause a different carbon cost at different locations or times.

6. Low-carbon price-based demand response

The paper embeds carbon cost directly into the user’s energy price.

The effective energy price contains:

Energy Attribute Price (EAP) + carbon-price component

If a user’s emissions remain within its carbon cap:

the carbon-price component is zero.

If emissions exceed the cap:

the carbon price increases the effective electricity/gas price.

Users therefore respond not only to energy price, but also to the carbon intensity of their consumption.

This creates a direct link:

carbon intensity → user price → demand response → emissions

7. Flexible loads considered

The demand-side response contains two important types of flexibility.

Time-shiftable loads

Energy consumption can be moved from one time period to another while maintaining the total required energy.

This is useful for:

  • avoiding high-carbon periods;
  • absorbing renewable energy;
  • reducing carbon-trading cost.

Convertible loads

Some demand can switch between electricity and natural gas.

For example:

electricity demand ↔︎ gas demand

This allows users to choose the lower-carbon energy carrier depending on the current system condition.

So the paper does not only reduce demand.

It also changes:

when energy is consumed + which energy carrier is used.

8. Upper-level innovation: customized step-based carbon prices

A standard step-based carbon tariff may use the same steps for every user.

The paper argues that this is inefficient because MELAs have very different:

  • consumption levels;
  • carbon intensities;
  • emissions.

It therefore customizes the step structure for each MELA.

Below the carbon cap:

\[ \tau^{pen}=0 \]

Above the cap, the carbon price increases step by step as emissions rise.

The optimization decides both:

  • the emission range covered by each step;
  • the carbon price of each step.

The goal is to balance:

strong emission-reduction incentives ↔︎ user affordability / social welfare

9. Why customized steps are better than uniform steps

Suppose two MELAs have different carbon intensities.

A uniform price structure treats them identically.

But the same price signal can be:

  • too weak for a high-emission user;
  • unnecessarily expensive for a relatively clean user.

The customized model can instead create different:

  • low-price ranges;
  • price increments;
  • penalty slopes.

This makes the demand-side carbon policy more targeted.

A key point from the results is that high-carbon users can receive a wider low-price interval but steeper later price increments.

This reduces excessive initial burden while still strongly discouraging very high emissions.

10. Lower-level market: competitive auction

In the lower-level market, MELA-buyers and MELA-sellers trade CERs voluntarily.

The auction gives priority to:

  • sellers with more excess CERs;
  • buyers with higher bids.

A buyer can increase its bid to improve its trading priority.

However, its bid cannot exceed the carbon price it would otherwise face in the upper-level market.

This creates a natural ceiling:

if lower-level CERs become too expensive, the buyer simply purchases the remaining rights from the upper-level regulator.

11. Optimal carbon-trading strategy of a MELA

Each MELA does more than submit a bid.

It also adjusts its energy consumption while participating in the auction.

The objective is to maximize consumer surplus:

\[ \text{consumer utility} - \text{energy cost} - \text{carbon-trading cost} + \text{flexibility incentives} \]

So carbon trading and energy use are jointly optimized.

A buyer can respond to a high CER price by:

  • reducing consumption;
  • shifting loads;
  • converting electricity demand to gas or vice versa;
  • buying CERs in the lower-level market;
  • buying remaining CERs from the upper-level market.

12. Source-side low-carbon dispatch is still retained

The demand-side carbon market does not replace source-side carbon control.

The paper combines both.

The IEGS operator performs low-carbon dispatch considering:

  • coal-fired units;
  • natural-gas units;
  • wind;
  • gas wells;
  • gas storage;
  • Carbon Capture Power Plant (CCPP);
  • Power-to-Gas (P2G);
  • flexible demand.

This is important because demand-side behavior changes:

  • electricity demand;
  • gas demand;
  • renewable-energy accommodation;
  • generator dispatch;
  • node carbon intensity.

So source and demand decisions are strongly coupled.

13. Role of CCPP and P2G

Carbon Capture Power Plant (CCPP)

A CCPP can decouple gross generation from net carbon emission by consuming part of its electricity for carbon capture.

The paper allows the carbon-capture process to shift its power use across time.

Power-to-Gas (P2G)

P2G converts electricity into gas.

It can absorb excess wind generation and increase electricity-gas coupling flexibility.

Together, CCPP and P2G support low-carbon operation on the source side while the carbon market changes behavior on the demand side.

14. Solution architecture: ADMM + auction + Benders

The overall model contains several interacting optimization layers.

Three main solution tools are used.

ADMM in the upper-level source-demand coordination

The Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) coordinates:

  • the IEGS operator;
  • demand-side MELAs.

The source side and demand side solve their own subproblems and exchange only coupling variables such as electricity/gas quantities.

This helps protect private information.

Competitive auction in the lower-level carbon market

MELAs repeatedly update:

  • CER demand;
  • bid price;
  • energy consumption.

Benders decomposition across the bi-level model

The upper-level model is treated as a master problem.

The lower-level carbon-trading model acts as a subproblem.

Benders cuts are added until the demand decisions of the two levels are consistent.

15. Main case-study design

The illustrative system contains:

  • a 5-bus power system;
  • a 7-node gas system;
  • 4 MELAs;
  • coal and gas generators;
  • wind generation;
  • a CCPP;
  • a P2G facility.

The paper compares five main carbon-control schemes.

Case 1

Traditional source-side carbon cap-and-trade only.

Case 2

Demand-side bi-level carbon market only.

Case 3

Source-side carbon trading + demand-side upper market with uniform step-based carbon prices.

Case 4

Source-side carbon trading + customized upper-level step-based carbon prices.

Case 5

Source-side carbon trading + full proposed bi-level demand-side carbon market.

Case 5 represents the complete proposed framework.

16. Main result: source-side or demand-side control alone is not enough

Reported total emissions are:

  • Case 1: 21,055.66 t
  • Case 2: 20,447.13 t
  • Case 5: 14,764.12 t

Case 5 achieves the lowest emission.

The logic is clear:

  • source-side carbon trading changes generation;
  • demand-side trading changes consumption;
  • combining both attacks the problem from both directions.

The paper also reports that Case 5 provides the highest social welfare among the compared schemes.

17. What happens in the lower-level carbon market

In Case 5:

  • MELA-1 and MELA-4 are CER sellers;
  • MELA-2 and MELA-3 are CER buyers.

MELA-2 faces a relatively high upper-level carbon price.

It therefore bids aggressively in the lower-level market to obtain cheaper CERs from sellers.

MELA-3 cannot obtain enough CERs from the lower-level market and must still buy some rights from the upper-level regulator.

The total carbon-trading cost of MELA-2 and MELA-3 is reported as:

$51,037.42

which is lower than the corresponding cost under the simpler step-price cases.

18. Customized carbon prices change incentives

The paper compares uniform and customized step-based carbon prices.

The customized prices are linked to each MELA’s carbon intensity.

This allows the market to:

  • reduce financial pressure on users;
  • maintain stronger penalties at high emission levels;
  • improve the acceptability of demand-side carbon trading.

However, customized upper-level prices alone are not enough.

Case 4 reduces the financial burden but weakens the incentive to reduce emissions.

The lower-level trading market in Case 5 is therefore needed to restore a stronger economic incentive.

19. CCPP and demand-side carbon trading reinforce each other

The paper compares Case 5 with a Case 6 in which the CCPP is removed.

Results:

  • Case 5 emissions: 14,764.12 t
  • Case 6 emissions: 20,914.41 t

Carbon-trading cost:

  • Case 5: $51,037.42
  • Case 6: $94,352.10

So source-side low-carbon technology and demand-side carbon trading are complementary.

The demand response reduces and reshapes energy use, giving the CCPP more opportunity to capture carbon economically.

At the same time, the CCPP lowers system carbon intensity and reduces users’ carbon cost.

20. How flexible demand changes operation

Time-shiftable loads

Electricity and gas loads are shifted:

from peak/high-emission periods → valley/lower-emission periods

Convertible loads

During high-wind periods:

gas demand can be converted toward electricity demand to absorb more wind power.

When wind output decreases:

electricity demand can shift toward gas.

So the proposed carbon mechanism actively changes the temporal and cross-energy structure of demand.

The paper tests:

  • 4 steps;
  • 6 steps;
  • 8 steps.

As the number of steps increases:

  • the step tariff better approximates a continuous carbon-price curve;
  • upper-level carbon-trading cost decreases;
  • computation time increases.

So there is a clear trade-off:

price accuracy ↔︎ computational complexity

22. Auction bid increment also creates a trade-off

A small bid increment gives buyers more precise control over the final CER price.

But it requires more auction rounds.

The paper finds that when the bid increment becomes very small, computation time rises quickly while trading-cost improvement becomes minor.

For the tested case, an increment of about:

$0.1

is suggested as a reasonable compromise between economic performance and auction efficiency.

23. User willingness to reduce emissions matters

The paper uses parameter \(\beta\) to represent a user’s willingness to change energy consumption for emission mitigation.

A larger \(\beta\) means stronger willingness.

As \(\beta\) increases from 0.01 to 0.05:

  • emissions decrease from 14,764.12 t to 13,278.65 t;
  • total CER purchasing cost decreases from $51,037.42 to $32,853.91.

This shows that carbon-market performance depends not only on technology and prices.

It also depends on behavioral response.

24. Large-scale test

The paper also tests a larger electricity-gas system based on the Xinjiang power grid.

The power side contains:

  • 41 coal-fired units;
  • 5 natural-gas units;
  • 16 wind farms;
  • 3 CCPPs;
  • 3 P2G facilities.

The gas side contains:

  • 6 gas wells;
  • 4 compressors;
  • 3 gas-storage facilities.

The proposed scheme still improves total cost and social welfare in the large-scale case.

25. Convergence and computation

The outer Benders algorithm converges in approximately:

  • 5 iterations for the illustrative system;
  • 13 iterations for the large-scale system.

Reported total computation time:

  • illustrative system: 86.21 s
  • large-scale system: 555.00 s

The authors argue that this is acceptable for:

  • day-ahead scheduling;
  • carbon-policy analysis.

It is not designed as a second-by-second real-time controller.

26. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Carbon responsibility can be assigned to demand as well as generation

Physical emissions occur at generators, but demand drives generation.

Demand-side carbon pricing makes this responsibility visible to users.

Lesson 2 — A carbon market is more powerful when users can both pay and earn

Low-emission users can sell CERs.

This gives users a positive financial reason to reduce emissions.

Lesson 3 — Uniform carbon prices can be economically unfair

Users with different carbon intensities and energy needs may require different price structures.

Lesson 4 — Demand response and carbon trading should be designed together

Carbon prices affect energy consumption.

Energy consumption changes generation and network carbon intensity.

So carbon trading cannot be treated independently from energy-system operation.

Lesson 5 — Source-side technology and demand-side market design are complementary

CCPP/P2G and demand-side carbon trading reinforce one another rather than acting as substitutes.

27. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • uncertainty in renewable generation, load, and prices is not modeled explicitly;
  • user response is represented through predetermined elasticity and utility parameters;
  • the carbon-pricing model is step-based rather than fully continuous;
  • the competitive auction assumes the proposed market rules and rational response behavior;
  • the main formulation is day-ahead rather than rolling or real time;
  • strategic behavior of the IEGS operator is not the main focus;
  • detailed market power and collusion in the carbon auction are not studied;
  • the model is computationally complex because it combines ADMM, auction iteration, and Benders decomposition.

The authors specifically identify a more detailed continuous piecewise carbon-price model as future work.

28. Research idea triggers

Possible extensions include:

  • stochastic or Distributionally Robust Optimization under wind/load uncertainty;
  • dynamic or continuous carbon pricing instead of discrete steps;
  • learning user-specific carbon-demand elasticity from real data;
  • carbon-market power and strategic bidding by large MELAs;
  • rolling-horizon electricity-gas-carbon co-optimization;
  • carbon trading with battery, EV, and thermal-storage flexibility;
  • electricity + gas + carbon + green-certificate joint markets;
  • privacy-preserving carbon trading;
  • decentralized demand-side carbon markets without a central auction exchange;
  • mechanism design considering fairness among heterogeneous users;
  • carbon-aware Virtual Power Plant (VPP) operation;
  • demand-side carbon trading for industrial parks, campuses, or data centers.

注记P017 — Carbon-Aware Peer-to-Peer Energy Trading in an Unbalanced Distribution Network via a Nash Equilibrium Discovery Deep Reinforcement Learning Approach

Author: Xiao Liu, Yujian Ye, Sinan Li, Cuo Zhang, Qianyi Ma, and Jianguo Zhu
Date: 2025
Categories: peer-to-peer energy trading; carbon trading; unbalanced distribution network; carbon emission flow; multi-agent deep reinforcement learning; Nash equilibrium

Bibliographic information

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 16(4), 3392–3407, July 2025
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2025.3561515

One-sentence takeaway

This paper builds a joint peer-to-peer electricity and carbon-permit market for a three-phase unbalanced distribution network, traces carbon emissions at the nodal-phase level, and uses a Nash-equilibrium-discovery multi-agent TRPO framework to learn bidding and local energy-management strategies under stochastic market conditions.

1. What problem does the paper solve?

Most network-constrained P2P energy-trading studies simplify the physical grid as a single-phase equivalent network. That approximation is convenient, but it can become problematic when prosumers are connected to different phases and their distributed resources create phase-dependent power flows and carbon intensities.

The paper therefore addresses four coupled issues:

  1. Network accuracy: P2P trading should respect a three-phase unbalanced distribution network rather than a single-phase approximation.
  2. Carbon accounting: carbon emissions should be traced from generators to prosumers according to the actual power-flow pattern.
  3. Joint market operation: electricity and carbon permits should be traded together without relying on a fully centralized market-clearing model.
  4. Strategic bidding: prosumers need a data-driven bidding method that can cope with stochastic and partially known market dynamics and converge to a stable strategic outcome.

The key research question can be summarized as:

How can prosumers trade both electricity and carbon permits
while the DSO simultaneously guarantees network security,
allocates emissions fairly in an unbalanced network,
and provides economically meaningful price signals?

2. The three-layer market architecture

The proposed framework contains three layers: a physical layer, a P2P trading market layer, and a coordination layer.

Physical layer

Each prosumer is connected to a specific bus and phase of the unbalanced distribution network and may own distributed resources such as:

  • renewable generation,
  • microturbines,
  • flexible demand,
  • local loads.

The network itself is represented by a three-phase branch-flow model.

P2P trading market layer

Each prosumer submits two sets of bids:

Electricity bid = quantity + price
Carbon-permit bid = quantity + price

The market is cleared through a continuous double auction (CDA). Electricity and carbon permits are traded separately but within the same joint electricity-and-carbon trading framework.

A useful feature is that the two trading roles are independent. A prosumer can simultaneously be, for example:

Electricity seller
+
Carbon-permit buyer

or the reverse.

Coordination layer

The DSO sits above the P2P market and performs three main tasks:

  • ensures safe operation of the unbalanced network,
  • performs carbon-emission tracing and accounting,
  • provides electricity and carbon service prices to prosumers.

The interaction between the DSO and prosumers is formulated as a bi-level coordination problem.

3. Upper level: carbon-aware operation of the unbalanced distribution network

The DSO first solves an unbalanced optimal power-flow problem. The model contains:

  • three-phase active/reactive power balances,
  • voltage-drop equations,
  • voltage limits,
  • second-order-cone current constraints.

The formulation retains phase-specific variables and phase coupling, although a near-balanced voltage-angle approximation is introduced to obtain a tractable branch-flow representation.

The DSO objective includes:

excess carbon-permit purchase cost
+
upstream electricity purchase cost
+
network-loss cost

This becomes the paper’s carbon-aware optimal power flow (CAOPF) model.

4. Carbon emission flow is extended to the nodal-phase level

A major contribution is the extension of carbon emission flow (CEF) from a conventional nodal formulation to a nodal-phase formulation.

Instead of assigning one carbon intensity to an entire bus, the paper calculates:

carbon intensity at bus j, phase a
carbon intensity at bus j, phase b
carbon intensity at bus j, phase c

The carbon intensity depends on:

  • locally injected generation,
  • carbon intensity of local generation technologies,
  • incoming line power flow,
  • line losses,
  • carbon intensity transported through upstream lines.

The basic logic is:

power flow determines where electricity comes from
        ↓
carbon emission flow follows the power flow
        ↓
carbon intensity is allocated at each node and phase
        ↓
prosumer carbon emissions are calculated from its actual phase connection

This is especially important for prosumers whose role can change between net consumer and net producer.

5. Why single-phase carbon accounting can be unfair

The paper explicitly demonstrates two possible errors when a three-phase network is replaced by a single-phase approximation.

Over-allocation

If a high-carbon generator and a load are connected at the same bus but on different phases, the single-phase approximation may make the load appear more carbon intensive than it really is.

Result:

the prosumer is assigned too much carbon responsibility and pays too much.

Under-allocation

If low-carbon renewable generation is located at the same bus but another phase, averaging phases can dilute the true carbon intensity.

Result:

the prosumer may be assigned too little carbon responsibility, while the DSO under-recovers the corresponding carbon cost.

This is one of the strongest physical-network motivations of the paper: carbon allocation is not only a market-design problem; it can also be a phase-level power-flow problem.

6. DLMPE and DLMPC: two locational price signals

The CAOPF produces two key price signals.

DLMPE

Distribution Locational Marginal Price for Electricity represents the location- and phase-dependent electricity service price.

DLMPC

Distribution Locational Marginal Price for Carbon represents the location- and phase-dependent carbon service price.

DLMPC is derived from the marginal value of the carbon-permit constraint and is further scaled by the local carbon intensity.

The intuitive result is:

higher local carbon intensity
        ↓
stronger carbon price signal
        ↓
stronger incentive to adjust local operation or P2P trading

Thus, carbon intensity is explicitly externalized as a price signal rather than remaining only an accounting indicator.

7. Lower level: what each prosumer optimizes

Each prosumer minimizes:

P2P trading cost
+
peer-to-grid settlement cost
+
local operating cost

The local operating decisions include:

  • microturbine output,
  • demand-response quantity,
  • electricity bidding price,
  • carbon-permit bidding price.

The net electricity bid is determined by:

load
− demand response
− renewable generation
− microturbine generation

The carbon-permit bid is determined by:

actual prosumer carbon emissions
− allocated local carbon cap

Therefore:

positive carbon bid quantity  → carbon-permit buyer
negative carbon bid quantity  → carbon-permit seller

8. Why use a continuous double auction?

The CDA market continuously matches buyers and sellers according to price bids.

For each auction round:

  1. buyers are ordered from high to low bidding price;
  2. sellers are ordered from low to high asking price;
  3. a trade occurs when the buyer’s bid is no lower than the seller’s ask;
  4. the cleared quantity is the smaller of the remaining buy/sell quantities;
  5. the transaction price is the midpoint of buyer and seller prices.

Unmatched electricity is settled with the DSO using either the DLMPE or feed-in tariff. Carbon-permit deficits can also be purchased from the DSO, but surplus carbon permits are not sold back to the DSO.

This arrangement creates a natural economic band for P2P prices:

P2P electricity price:
FiT  ≤ bid price ≤ mean DLMPE

P2P carbon price:
0    ≤ bid price ≤ mean DLMPC

9. Why the authors use multi-agent reinforcement learning

The joint electricity-carbon CDA market is stochastic and changes after every bidding round. Each prosumer does not know the exact future decisions of the other prosumers or the exact DSO prices in advance.

A conventional optimization model would require detailed information about:

  • all prosumers,
  • DSO operation,
  • rivals’ bidding behavior,
  • future market-clearing prices.

The authors therefore reformulate the market interaction as a cooperative Markov game.

Reward

For each prosumer:

reward = − total operating and trading cost

The joint reward is the sum of all prosumers’ rewards.

Action space

Each agent chooses:

microturbine dispatch
+demand response
+electricity bid price
+carbon bid price

Observation space

The observation includes:

  • local nodal-phase carbon intensity,
  • local generation carbon intensity,
  • load,
  • renewable generation,
  • previous electricity/carbon bids,
  • previous DSO-cleared electricity/carbon prices.

10. The key algorithmic contribution: Nash-equilibrium-discovery TRPO

The paper argues that simply applying MADRL is not enough. In a bidding game, a useful stable solution should satisfy a Nash-equilibrium-type condition:

when other agents keep their policies fixed, no individual prosumer should be able to improve its return by unilaterally changing its own policy.

The proposed method is therefore named NE-TRPO.

The authors use a multi-agent advantage decomposition and sequential policy update rule inspired by mirror learning.

The basic idea is:

update prosumer 1
        ↓
prosumer 2 updates while considering prosumer 1's new policy
        ↓
prosumer 3 updates while considering prosumers 1 and 2
        ↓
randomize update order in later iterations
        ↓
approach a fixed point where unilateral policy changes are no longer beneficial

TRPO provides a trust-region constraint through KL divergence, limiting the policy update magnitude and improving training stability.

11. How the two levels interact

The paper’s Fig. 2 summarizes the complete information loop.

The upper level sends to the lower level:

  • DLMPE,
  • DLMPC,
  • nodal-phase carbon intensity.

The lower level sends back:

  • net active-power injection,
  • net local generation,
  • local generation carbon intensity.

During reinforcement-learning training, the prosumers are the agents, while the DSO/network/auction process is treated as part of the environment.

17. Critical reading and limitations

Several points are worth remembering when citing or extending the paper.

17.1 The unbalanced model is still an approximation

Although the paper is much more detailed than a single-phase equivalent model, it introduces an approximation that assumes phase-voltage ratios remain close to their balanced angular relationship. It is therefore not a fully nonlinear three-phase AC formulation.

17.2 Voltage unbalance factor is not explicitly constrained

The authors list incorporation of a voltage-unbalance-factor constraint as future work. Thus, phase-specific power flow is modeled, but explicit VUF compliance is not yet enforced.

17.3 Carbon caps are assumed rather than policy-calibrated

The local carbon cap is selected as a model input and explored through sensitivity analysis. A future implementation would need a principled allocation rule based on regulation, fairness, historical consumption, or emissions targets.

17.4 The NE result is tied to the training framework and assumptions

The theoretical discussion is stronger than in many MADRL trading papers, but the practical neural-network training process remains an approximation to the idealized mirror-learning/TRPO framework. It is useful to distinguish between a theoretically NE-discoverable update structure and an independently verified global equilibrium of the full nonlinear market environment.

17.5 Real-time information and privacy deserve further study

The approach still requires the DSO to calculate and broadcast phase-level carbon intensities and locational price signals. Communication delays, privacy leakage, cyber risks, and imperfect measurements would be natural extensions.

注记P018 — Charge Manipulation Attacks Against Smart Electric Vehicle Charging Stations and Deep Learning-Based Detection Mechanisms

Author: Hamidreza Jahangir, Subhash Lakshminarayana, and H. Vincent Poor
Date: 2024
Categories: electric vehicle charging; cybersecurity; charge manipulation attack; electricity market; OCPP; anomaly detection; deep autoencoder

Bibliographic information

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 15(5), 5182–5194, September 2024
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2024.3401090

One-sentence takeaway

This paper shows that a cyber attacker does not need to create an obvious high-frequency switching event to damage smart EV charging operations: by stealthily manipulating requested charging demand and preferred charging times, the attacker can shift the aggregator’s real-time demand away from its day-ahead schedule, increase market penalties and operating costs, and remain difficult to detect; the authors therefore propose an unsupervised 2D-CNN autoencoder monitoring framework for real-time CMA detection.

1. What problem does the paper solve?

Smart EV charging relies on communications between EV users, charging points/stations, central systems, mobile applications, and energy-market interfaces. This connectivity enables coordinated charging, but it also creates a cyber-physical attack surface.

Earlier EV charging cyber-security studies often focus on highly visible attacks such as:

  • sudden charging-demand surges,
  • synchronous switching of many chargers,
  • denial-of-service or false-data-injection attacks that directly disturb grid stability.

The authors argue that modern smart-charging protocols already include mechanisms that make some of these direct attacks harder to execute. They therefore ask a more practical question:

Can an attacker manipulate ordinary smart-charging preferences
without producing an obviously abnormal charging command,
and thereby create economically damaging deviations in the
energy market?

The paper proposes Charge Manipulation Attacks (CMAs) that modify charging-setting information such as requested demand, preferred start time, and preferred end time. The main impact is not necessarily an immediate grid collapse; instead, the attack changes the aggregator’s actual charging trajectory and therefore its day-ahead/real-time market settlement.

2. Why this attack is different from conventional EV charging attacks

The paper makes a useful distinction between three broad cyber-threat classes.

Sudden demand surge/drop attacks

These attacks attempt to turn on many chargers together or sharply change charging rates, potentially creating frequency or congestion problems.

Coordinated switching attacks

These repeatedly switch chargers on and off and can excite grid oscillations. They are potentially severe but require continued access and may be constrained by smart-charging control logic.

Energy-market manipulation

CMAs belong mainly to this third class. Their objective is to make the aggregator’s actual charging demand differ from its scheduled/forecast demand, creating:

forecast error
     ↓
DA/RT schedule mismatch
     ↓
additional RT purchases and/or penalties
     ↓
aggregator economic loss

This market-oriented perspective is one of the paper’s most important contributions.

3. OCPP context: why the authors consider CMAs more realistic

The paper uses the Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) architecture to explain the attack surface.

OCPP 1.6 and later versions contain smart-charging functions that can restrict maximum charging rates and introduce randomized delays. These features make extremely aggressive surge or rapid switching attacks less straightforward.

CMAs instead target ordinary charging-setting information, especially:

requested charging demand
preferred start time
preferred end time

The key idea is that manipulated settings can still look like plausible user requests. Therefore, the central system may have difficulty distinguishing malicious manipulation from normal variation in user charging behavior.

4. The six CMA types

The authors construct six attack categories from three basic manipulations.

Type 1 — requested-demand manipulation

The requested charging energy is increased while the charging window is otherwise maintained.

Main effect:

higher charging demand / higher peak demand.

Type 2 — demand increase + earlier end time

The requested demand is increased while the preferred end time is moved earlier.

Main effect:

charging demand is shifted toward earlier time periods.

Type 3 — demand increase + later start time

The requested demand is increased while the preferred start time is moved later.

Main effect:

charging demand is shifted toward later time periods.

Types 4–6 — mixed attacks

These combine Types 1–3 across different subsets of compromised charging points.

The important insight is that mixed attacks can be more stealthy because the aggregate charging-setting distribution is not altered in one simple direction. Instead, multiple small manipulations partly mask each other.

A useful summary is:

CMA type Main manipulation Main system effect Relative stealthiness reported by authors
Type 1 Requested demand Increase peak demand Lower
Type 2 Demand + earlier end time Shift demand earlier Lower
Type 3 Demand + later start time Shift demand later Lower
Type 4 Mix of Types 1 and 2 Increase + earlier shift Medium
Type 5 Mix of Types 1 and 3 Increase + later shift Medium
Type 6 Mix of Types 1–3 Mixed demand reshaping Highest

5. Why the attacks can be stealthy

The simulations use 5000 EV charging points and a five-minute resolution. For the illustrative case, approximately 70% of charging points are assumed compromised and the attack-induced increase in charging rate is kept relatively small.

The resulting charging-setting histograms before and after attack remain visually similar. This matters because a conventional rule such as:

if charging demand suddenly becomes extremely large → alarm

would not be sufficient.

Instead, the security problem becomes a multivariate anomaly-detection problem involving subtle changes across:

  • start time,
  • end time,
  • requested charging demand,
  • large numbers of charging points.

6. Why the electricity market is vulnerable

The paper models an EV aggregator participating in both the day-ahead market (DAM) and real-time market (RTM).

The aggregator first predicts the next day’s charging demand and places DA bids. In real time, it must operate close to the awarded DA schedule. If actual charging demand changes unexpectedly, it may need to:

  • submit incremental RT bids,
  • pay for deviations,
  • incur penalties for not honoring its DA commitments,
  • suffer energy-not-served costs.

Therefore, a CMA can create an economic attack even when the power system remains operationally stable.

The main chain is:

CMA changes user charging settings
            ↓
smart-charging schedule changes
            ↓
actual RT demand deviates from DA forecast/bid
            ↓
RT balancing + penalty cost rises
            ↓
aggregator profit and market credibility deteriorate

7. Energy-market cost model

The authors represent the aggregator’s total cost as:

Total Cost = Day-Ahead Cost + Real-Time Cost

The day-ahead component includes:

DA charging cost
+
expected energy-not-charged cost

The real-time component includes:

incremental RT bidding cost
+
DA-bid deviation penalty
+
real-time energy-not-charged cost

This formulation is important because the cyberattack is translated into a directly measurable economic consequence instead of only a grid-stability metric.

8. Type-1 attack result: about 13% higher surcharge cost

The paper uses CAISO market prices and the ACN-data EV charging dataset to quantify the market impact.

For the reported Type-1 CMA case:

The economic damage comes from the overall mismatch between planned and actual charging behavior.

9. Mixed CMAs are economically more damaging

The boxplots for the six CMA scenarios show that Types 4–6 generally lead to higher surcharge costs than Types 1–3.

simple CMA

This is especially important because the mixed attacks are also harder to detect, creating a difficult combination of:

Instead of requiring additional physical sensors, the paper proposes using data already available in smart-charging platforms.

The central monitoring system collects changes in:

requested charging demand

for large populations of EV charging points.

The monitoring architecture is intended to operate as an additional protection layer alongside conventional encryption/authentication mechanisms.

11. Why use unsupervised anomaly detection?

Cyberattack datasets are usually highly imbalanced and labeled attack examples are limited. Moreover, future attacks may not exactly match known attack patterns.

The authors therefore choose an unsupervised autoencoder.

The model is trained mainly on normal charging behavior:

normal charging profiles
        ↓
encoder
        ↓
latent representation
        ↓
decoder
        ↓
reconstructed charging profiles

If a new sample is normal, the autoencoder should reconstruct it accurately.

If a new sample contains an abnormal pattern:

reconstruction error ↑
        ↓
loss exceeds threshold
        ↓
anomaly alarm

This avoids requiring a fully labeled training set containing every possible CMA type.

12. 2D-CNN autoencoder monitoring framework

The main detection architecture uses a 2D convolutional autoencoder.

For each monitoring interval, the input is based on the change in charging settings between two consecutive time points.

Conceptually:

Δ requested demand
Δ start time
Δ end time
        ↓

        ↓
2D-CNN autoencoder
        ↓
reconstruction loss
        ↓
normal / anomalous

The paper chooses the anomaly threshold from the loss distribution of normal validation samples and uses a 5% false-positive tolerance in threshold selection.

  • a deep MLP autoencoder,
  • One-Class SVM,
  • Isolation Forest.

The offline model training is reported to take less than 17 minutes on the authors’ test computer, while online inference takes approximately 96 ms, indicating that the method is compatible with near-real-time monitoring.

15. Why one-time manipulation matters

The authors emphasize that the proposed CMA does not require continuous high-frequency control of the charging devices.

This has three implications.

Greater stealthiness

Repeated switching creates an obvious temporal signature. A one-time manipulation of a charging request can instead be absorbed by the legitimate smart-charging scheduler.

Lower attacker persistence requirement

The attacker does not need to maintain uninterrupted control throughout the entire charging process.

Greater market relevance

Even a small manipulation made before or during market operation can change the aggregate schedule for many subsequent time intervals.

This changes the cyber-risk question from:

Can an attacker directly switch many chargers?

to:

Can an attacker subtly corrupt the input information
used by the legitimate charging scheduler?

That is a much broader vulnerability class.

17. Critical reading and limitations

Several limitations are worth remembering.

17.1 Market model is a generalized CAISO representation

The authors explicitly state that they do not replicate every detailed CAISO penalty and real-time settlement rule. Therefore, the reported dollar values demonstrate the mechanism and relative impact rather than serving as exact market-loss forecasts.

17.2 Detection is evaluated on synthetically generated attacks

Normal behavior comes from a real EV charging dataset, but attack samples are generated by the proposed CMA procedure. Real attackers may produce different statistical signatures.

17.3 Aggregate accuracy hides attack-specific weaknesses

The overall 91.24% accuracy looks strong, but Type-6 detection is much weaker. For a security application, worst-case or attack-specific recall may be more important than aggregate accuracy.

17.4 The threshold is fixed from normal validation data

A fixed anomaly threshold may require recalibration when charging behavior, fleet composition, seasons, tariffs, or user populations change.

17.5 Detection is emphasized more than mitigation

The paper focuses on detecting attacks. A practical resilient system would also need a response policy, for example:

  • isolate suspicious charging requests,
  • revalidate user preferences,
  • re-optimize the charging schedule,
  • reserve flexibility for cyber contingencies,
  • quantify confidence before rejecting legitimate requests.

17.6 Communication imperfections remain future work

The authors identify missing and noisy monitoring data as an important future issue. This is highly relevant because a cyber-monitoring system itself depends on reliable communication.

P018:

For future P2P/VPP/EV-market research, it suggests that market models should not automatically assume that every submitted demand, flexibility, or preference signal is trustworthy.

注记[P019] Wu et al. (2025) — Consensus Algorithm-Based Two-Stage Frequency Regulation Strategy With EVs Participating as VSMs

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 16(2), 1562–1574, 2025
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2024.3507012
Main topics: Electric vehicles; Virtual Synchronous Machine (VSM); frequency regulation; isolated grid; inertial response; primary frequency control; secondary frequency control; day-ahead economic dispatch; real-time consensus algorithm; charging stations; battery swapping stations

Quick takeaway

This paper investigates how large populations of EVs can support the frequency stability of an isolated grid with high renewable penetration.

The central idea is to make EV charging stations (CSs) and battery swapping stations (BSSs) behave like Virtual Synchronous Machines (VSMs) rather than ordinary converter-connected loads.

The control architecture then combines two time scales:

  • Day-ahead stage (DAS): solve an economic dispatch problem to obtain an operating baseline for generation units, CSs, and BSSs.
  • Real-time stage (RTS): use an electricity-price-guided consensus algorithm to allocate frequency-regulation power among multiple units.

The result is a coordinated scheme that tries to achieve both:

frequency stability + economic operation.

1. Problem addressed

Isolated grids are especially vulnerable to frequency disturbances because they have:

  • limited generation capacity;
  • no support from a large interconnected system;
  • relatively large load variations;
  • increasing shares of inverter-based renewable generation;
  • lower effective rotational inertia.

At the same time, large numbers of EVs are connected through power-electronic converters.

Conventional converter-connected EV charging does not naturally provide the electromechanical inertia of synchronous machines.

The paper therefore asks:

Can EV charging infrastructure be controlled so that it contributes both active-power regulation and virtual rotational inertia, while still operating economically?

2. Why EVs are attractive frequency-regulation resources

EV charging infrastructure is flexible because charging power can be adjusted over short time scales.

When system frequency is below nominal:

  • generators can increase output;
  • CSs/BSSs can reduce charging demand;
  • bidirectional charging resources can increase discharge.

When system frequency is above nominal:

  • generators can decrease output;
  • CSs/BSSs can increase charging demand;
  • discharge can be reduced.

Thus EVs can act as controllable demand-side frequency-regulation resources.

However, using EVs only as fast controllable loads does not solve the declining-inertia problem.

That motivates the use of VSM control.

3. Why VSM control is introduced

A conventional EV frequency-response model mainly follows a power command.

This gives fast active-power control, but the response is largely independent of the physical frequency dynamics of the grid.

The proposed VSM-based model instead imitates the swing behavior of a synchronous machine.

Its basic virtual inertia equation is:

\[ J\frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{P_m-P_e}{\omega} -D(\omega-\omega_g) \]

where:

  • \(J\): virtual rotational inertia;
  • \(D\): damping coefficient;
  • \(\omega\): virtual angular frequency;
  • \(\omega_g\): measured grid angular frequency;
  • \(P_m\): virtual mechanical power;
  • \(P_e\): electromagnetic power.

The mechanical-power reference is also equipped with droop behavior:

\[ P_m=P_{ref}+K_f(\omega_0-\omega_g) \]

So EV charging infrastructure can respond to both:

frequency deviation + power command.

4. The isolated-grid system considered

The system-frequency-response model contains several types of resources.

Generation side:

  • Biogas Power Plant (BPP);
  • Small Hydropower Plants (SHPs);
  • Run-off Hydropower Plants (RHPs);
  • wind farm;
  • photovoltaic power station.

Demand / flexible side:

  • EV Charging Stations (CSs);
  • Battery Swapping Stations (BSSs).

Wind and PV are connected through inverters, while CSs and BSSs are connected through the proposed VSM-based converters.

This creates a useful test bed for studying a low-inertia isolated grid with both renewable generation and flexible EV demand.

5. Proposed VSM-based frequency-response model

After linearization and Laplace transformation, the change in EV-side VSM power is related to both frequency change and reference-power change.

The paper derives:

\[ \Delta P_{e1}(s)= \frac{J\omega_0S_Vs+K_fS_V} {J\omega_0s^2+Ds+S_V} \Delta\omega_g(s) \]

and

\[ \Delta P_{e2}(s)= \frac{S_V} {J\omega_0s^2+Ds+S_V} \Delta P_{ref}(s) \]

where:

\[ S_V=\frac{EV_g}{X_f} \]

This makes the VSM-FR model a second-order dynamic system rather than a simple first-order command-following model.

6. What virtual inertia means here

The EVs do not contain rotating masses that are mechanically synchronized with the grid.

The inertia is therefore emulated through converter control.

When frequency falls, the VSM-controlled CS/BSS reduces its power consumption according to the frequency response.

When frequency rises, it increases consumption.

The resulting converter response mimics part of the inertial and droop behavior of a synchronous generator.

So the paper is not claiming that EV batteries physically create rotating inertia.

Instead, they provide:

synthetic / virtual inertia through active-power modulation.

7. Main architecture: two-stage frequency regulation

The complete method is divided into two scheduling stages.

Day-ahead stage

Forecast:

  • wind generation;
  • PV generation;
  • system load.

Then solve a multi-unit economic dispatch model.

This creates the baseline operating schedule.

Real-time stage

The actual system will deviate from the forecast because of:

  • renewable uncertainty;
  • load fluctuations;
  • real-time disturbances.

A consensus network therefore reallocates regulation power among the available units in real time.

The two stages are designed to work together rather than independently.

8. Why day-ahead dispatch is needed before real-time consensus

A purely real-time consensus algorithm can react quickly, but it does not necessarily consider the full 24-hour economic context.

For example, if it only reacts to the current regulation price, it may ignore:

  • high-price periods later in the day;
  • storage-like constraints of hydropower reservoirs;
  • biogas availability;
  • operating limits of CS/BSS resources.

The day-ahead economic dispatch therefore provides a coarse optimal operating trajectory.

The real-time consensus controller then performs fine corrections around this trajectory.

The structure is essentially:

day-ahead economic baseline → real-time frequency correction.

9. Day-ahead economic dispatch model

The day-ahead objective minimizes the total system operating cost:

\[ \min C^{grid} \]

with cost contributions from:

  • RHPs;
  • SHP;
  • BPP;
  • charging stations;
  • battery swapping stations;
  • system load.

The model also includes:

  • biogas storage and production constraints;
  • hydropower flow constraints;
  • reservoir constraints;
  • CS/BSS operating-power constraints;
  • system-wide power balance.

The authors solve the optimization using:

YALMIP + CPLEX in MATLAB.

10. Economic modeling of CSs and BSSs

For a charging station, the daily cost is modeled as electricity-purchase cost minus charging-service income:

\[ C_i^{CS} = \sum_t (\lambda_t^{buy}-\alpha_{i,t}^{sell})P_{i,t}^{CS} \]

A similar structure is used for BSSs.

This is important because the EV infrastructure is not treated merely as a technical frequency-control device.

Its own commercial operation is explicitly represented.

That makes the real-time regulation problem an economic allocation problem as well as a control problem.

11. Real-time consensus network

In the RTS, all controllable units form a communication graph.

The paper assigns:

  • the BPP as leader;
  • the remaining units as followers.

Each unit exchanges information only with neighboring agents.

The consensus variable is the regulation cost.

The aim is to distribute the required frequency-regulation power so that units with more favorable real-time regulation economics take a larger share of the command.

This gives the scheme a distributed rather than purely centralized character.

12. Regulation cost signal

For a generator, the regulation cost is represented approximately as:

\[ r_{i,t}=c_{i,t}\Delta P_{i,t} \]

For CSs and BSSs, the unit regulation price includes:

  • electricity purchase price;
  • regulation-market benefit;
  • charging-service revenue.

So the dispatch priority is not based only on available power capacity.

It depends on the marginal economic effect of regulation at that moment.

A lower regulation unit price leads to a larger allocated regulation power.

13. Why price normalization is necessary

Real-time electricity and regulation prices can cause the effective unit regulation cost to become negative.

A conventional consensus allocation method may not work properly when the allocation index crosses zero.

The paper therefore transforms the real-time regulation price using:

\[ c'_{i,t} = \frac{c_{i,t}-\min(C_t)} {\Phi[\max(C_t)-\min(C_t)]} \]

This transformation:

  • maps the regulation-price signal to positive values;
  • preserves the relative ordering among units;
  • allows the consensus allocation rule to remain valid even when the original market price is negative.

This is one of the practical modifications made to the standard consensus method.

14. Dispatchable capacity is still respected

Economic preference alone cannot determine the allocation.

Each unit has physical frequency-regulation limits.

For generators, up/down regulation is limited by available generating headroom.

For CSs and BSSs, the available regulation depends on their current operating power and charging/discharging limits.

Importantly, CS/BSS capacity is updated in real time because EV connection and charging behavior are uncertain.

Thus the final allocation combines:

economic priority + real-time dispatchable capacity.

15. Leader-follower consensus update

The leader receives the global regulation requirement and frequency information.

Its update includes:

  • mismatch between total command and allocated regulation power;
  • current frequency deviation;
  • neighboring-agent consensus information.

Followers update their regulation-cost states from neighboring units.

The conceptual structure is:

PI / frequency-regulation command
            ↓
          Leader
            ↓
   neighboring information
            ↓
       Followers
            ↓
 consensus on regulation cost
            ↓
  power command for each unit

This allows the system to redistribute the regulation requirement among heterogeneous resources.

16. Real-time communication and scheduling assumptions

The case study includes a communication delay of:

0.04 s per unit.

The RTS scheduling cycle is:

4 s.

The authors report that the consensus iteration is completed in approximately:

2 s,

leaving additional time margin within each scheduling cycle.

This is important because the method is intended for fast secondary / real-time frequency regulation rather than only hour-ahead scheduling.

17. Simulation system and data

The case study is based on an isolated grid associated with Qingyuan, Guangdong, China.

The model contains a 36-node system with:

  • BPP;
  • SHP;
  • four RHPs;
  • three charging stations;
  • two BSSs;
  • wind power;
  • PV generation;
  • system load.

The paper uses real-system data for:

  • load;
  • wind generation;
  • PV generation.

EV charging behavior is generated using Monte Carlo simulation based on historical operating data.

18. VSM model versus conventional EV frequency-response model

The paper compares:

  • IM-FR model: conventional individual EV frequency-response model;
  • VSM-FR model: proposed virtual-synchronous-machine response model.

Under a step power command, the conventional model follows the target more rapidly.

The VSM model rises more gradually because it intentionally reproduces second-order synchronous-machine-like dynamics.

This slower command response is not treated as a defect.

It is the mechanism through which the converter emulates inertia.

19. Response to frequency variations

The difference becomes clearer when frequency itself changes.

The conventional IM-FR response is mainly command-driven.

The VSM-FR model changes power according to:

  • frequency deviation;
  • rate of frequency change;
  • droop response.

Therefore, the VSM-controlled EVs contribute during the inertial-response stage before the slower frequency-regulation layers fully act.

This is the main technical reason why the authors report lower frequency excursions with the VSM interface.

20. Effect of increasing EV participation

The authors test cases with increasing EV access.

The qualitative trend is:

more EVs participating as VSMs → more controllable demand-side power → smaller frequency deviation.

The conclusion reports that when the number of participating EVs is increased by a factor of four:

  • the frequency nadir improves by approximately 33.3%;
  • the positive frequency peak decreases by approximately 41.2%.

This demonstrates that the benefit of the VSM approach increases with the aggregate EV capacity available for regulation.

21. Consensus convergence behavior

The paper also checks the iterative behavior of the real-time consensus network.

For a sample frequency-regulation command, the agents’ regulation costs converge and the allocated powers approach the requested total command.

The appendix further tests different initial scheduling costs and powers.

The reported errors for the example CS are below approximately:

0.1%.

So the authors use both numerical convergence and sensitivity to initial conditions to support the stability of the consensus iteration.

22. Four main comparison cases

The daily simulation compares four strategies.

Case 1 — Day-ahead ED only

Units follow the DAS schedule without real-time frequency-regulation correction.

Case 2 — Consensus regulation only

Units use the real-time consensus allocation without the proposed two-stage combination.

Case 3 — Day-ahead ED + equal allocation algorithm

The DAS schedule is combined with a conventional dispatchable-capacity-based Equal Allocation Algorithm (EAA).

Case 4 — Proposed method

The full method combines:

day-ahead ED + real-time price-guided consensus allocation + VSM-based EV participation.

23. Frequency-deviation comparison

Case 1 has the worst overall regulation performance because forecast error and renewable uncertainty cannot be corrected sufficiently in real time.

The largest reported deviation in Case 1 is approximately:

−0.034 Hz at 13:00.

Case 2 performs better during much of the day because consensus regulation reacts to real-time disturbances.

However, without the day-ahead baseline, a fixed-gain PI-based real-time scheme has difficulty following rapid load changes.

Cases 3 and 4 combine day-ahead and real-time scheduling and therefore perform more consistently.

Case 3 and Case 4 are particularly useful for judging the proposed real-time allocation rule.

Compared with Case 3:

  • average frequency deviation falls from 0.0071 Hz to 0.0044 Hz;
  • operating cost falls from 19,398.43 RMB to 15,579.30 RMB.

  • 38% reduction in average frequency deviation;
  • 19.69% reduction in operating cost.

This is the strongest evidence that the price-guided consensus allocation improves on a simple capacity-based equal-allocation strategy.

26. Why the proposed method works

The performance improvement comes from combining three mechanisms.

VSM control

Adds fast virtual inertia and droop-like support from EV charging infrastructure.

Day-ahead economic dispatch

Places the system near an economically reasonable operating point before disturbances occur.

Real-time consensus allocation

Redistributes regulation effort according to both:

  • current frequency needs;
  • real-time economic signals.

The paper therefore combines:

physical dynamics + scheduling + distributed economic coordination.

27. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — EVs can provide more than conventional demand response

With suitable converter control, EV charging resources can emulate synchronous-machine behavior and contribute to inertial response.

Lesson 2 — Frequency regulation is a multi-timescale problem

Day-ahead scheduling and second-level regulation should not be designed independently.

Lesson 3 — Dispatchable capacity alone is not enough for resource allocation

Two units with similar available regulation capacity may have very different real-time economic costs.

Lesson 4 — Consensus control can embed market signals

The consensus variable does not have to be only physical power; it can be constructed from economic regulation cost.

Lesson 5 — The best frequency strategy is not necessarily the cheapest strategy

The proposed method explicitly demonstrates the trade-off between system stability and operating economy.

28. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • the study is simulation-based rather than demonstrated through hardware-in-the-loop or field experiments;
  • EV availability and charging behavior are represented through Monte Carlo simulation rather than fully coupled travel-behavior uncertainty models;
  • battery degradation cost is not a major part of the economic objective;
  • user willingness and charging inconvenience caused by frequency regulation are not modeled in detail;
  • the VSM parameters are treated as predefined rather than optimally or adaptively tuned;
  • communication failures, packet loss, cyberattacks, and asynchronous updates are not extensively considered;
  • the real-time market and regulation-price model is simplified relative to a full ancillary-service market;
  • the network is modeled mainly through a single-area SFR framework rather than detailed AC power-flow constraints;
  • the study focuses on an isolated grid, so behavior in a large interconnected system is not directly demonstrated;
  • the interaction between thousands of individual EV battery constraints and aggregate CS/BSS-level VSM behavior could be modeled in greater detail.

注记[P020] He et al. (2026) — Coordinated Scheduling of EV Charging Service and Energy Arbitrage for Truck Mobile Charging Stations

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 17(1), 467–482, January 2026
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2025.3571790
Main topics: Truck Mobile Charging Station (TMCS); EV charging service; energy arbitrage; coordinated scheduling; charging-demand uncertainty; Multinomial Logit (MNL); extended graph model; Markov Decision Process (MDP); Approximate Dynamic Programming (ADP); Value Function Approximation (VFA); rolling horizon; LRH-VFA

Quick takeaway

This paper asks a practical business-model question for Truck Mobile Charging Stations (TMCSs):

If a large mobile battery truck is deployed mainly to support EV charging peaks, what should it do during low-demand periods instead of remaining idle?

The proposed answer is to coordinate two service provisions:

  • EV charging service: move to highway charging-demand hotspots and support overloaded fixed charging stations (FCSs);
  • energy arbitrage: when charging demand is low, connect to selected distribution-grid nodes, charge when electricity is cheap, and discharge when electricity is expensive.

The difficult part is that future EV charging demand is uncertain. A TMCS that performs too much energy arbitrage now may have insufficient energy or be in the wrong location when charging demand suddenly rises later.

The paper therefore proposes a lookahead rolling horizon-value function approximation (LRH-VFA) method. It combines:

  • offline learning from historical scenarios;
  • a piecewise-linear approximation of future value;
  • short-term lookahead forecasts;
  • online rolling optimization.

The central idea is:

do not maximize only the current-hour profit; attach an estimated future value to the current SOC and location decision.

1. Problem addressed

Conventional Fixed Charging Stations (FCSs) have several limitations:

  • expensive initial infrastructure investment;
  • limited ability to relocate or expand;
  • potentially severe congestion during holidays or peak travel periods;
  • low utilization in other periods;
  • possible coincidence between EV charging peaks and other electricity-demand peaks.

A TMCS is essentially:

truck + large battery storage + multiple EV chargers.

It can physically move between locations, which gives it much greater spatial flexibility than an FCS.

However, this flexibility creates a new economic problem.

If TMCS capacity is designed for peak EV demand, then on ordinary weekdays a large fraction of that expensive battery capacity may remain unused.

The paper therefore asks:

Can TMCSs switch dynamically between EV charging service and grid energy arbitrage so that the same asset earns revenue in both high-demand and low-demand periods?

2. Why charging-service-only operation can be economically weak

A TMCS is normally deployed because fixed infrastructure cannot economically cover every temporary demand peak.

But peak-oriented sizing creates idle capacity.

For example:

Holiday / peak traffic
        ↓
high EV charging demand
        ↓
TMCS mainly serves EVs

Ordinary weekday
        ↓
low EV charging demand
        ↓
TMCS may remain idle

If the truck, battery and chargers are expensive assets, low utilization directly weakens the business case.

The paper therefore treats asset utilization as a core economic issue rather than only a charging-service issue.

3. Two service provisions of TMCS

The TMCS has two main operating modes.

Service provision 1 — EV charging service

The TMCS travels to a charging-service node and supplies EV charging demand.

Its main role is to:

  • support FCSs;
  • absorb temporary charging peaks;
  • improve charging-service availability.

Service provision 2 — Energy arbitrage

During low EV charging demand, the TMCS can travel to an energy-arbitrage node in the distribution network.

It then:

  • charges during low-price periods;
  • discharges during high-price periods;
  • earns revenue from electricity-price differences.

So the same battery asset serves both:

transportation charging infrastructure + mobile grid energy storage.

4. The key coordination conflict

The two services compete for the same resources:

  • battery energy;
  • charging/discharging power;
  • location;
  • time;
  • battery life.

Energy arbitrage can increase short-term revenue, but excessive arbitrage may reduce the ability to satisfy later EV charging demand.

For example:

14:00
cheap electricity
→ attractive to charge for arbitrage

17:00
EV charging demand suddenly rises elsewhere
→ TMCS must travel there
→ battery energy and location now matter

Therefore, the decision at time t changes the feasible and profitable decisions at t+1, t+2, …

This is why a purely myopic optimizer is insufficient.

5. EV charging demand is not assumed to be fixed

A particularly useful feature of the paper is that EV charging demand for TMCS is generated from user behavior, rather than imposed directly as an exogenous load curve.

The model considers:

  • traffic origin-destination information;
  • EV departure time;
  • initial battery SOC;
  • trip path;
  • traffic speed;
  • road congestion;
  • outdoor temperature;
  • vehicle energy consumption;
  • charging fee;
  • charging time;
  • queuing time;
  • heterogeneous value of users’ time.

The resulting process is:

traffic + vehicle characteristics
          ↓
EV energy consumption
          ↓
need for charging
          ↓
set of feasible FCS/TMCS options
          ↓
user choice model
          ↓
spatial-temporal TMCS charging demand

6. Traffic and temperature affect EV energy consumption

The road network is represented by a directed graph.

EV routes are generated from OD information and the Floyd shortest-path algorithm.

Road speed is determined using a speed-flow relationship, so traffic congestion changes travel time.

The paper also explicitly models the impact of outdoor temperature on EV energy consumption through air-conditioning use.

This is important because charging demand is therefore not only a function of distance.

It is linked to:

traffic state + ambient temperature + battery state + travel plan.

7. How users choose between FCS and TMCS

When an EV needs charging, it may have several feasible charging options.

The user’s generalized charging cost includes:

  1. electricity charging fee;
  2. queuing-time value;
  3. actual charging-time value;
  4. additional service time.

The paper writes the cost of charging scheme j as:

\[ c_i(j) = \alpha_m^j M_i^{m,j} + \frac{INC_i}{T_m} \left( W_q^{j,t} + \frac{M_i^{m,j}}{\eta_{dch}P_r^j} +s_c \right) \]

The Multinomial Logit (MNL) model is then used to simulate the probability that an EV driver selects a given FCS/TMCS charging option.

As a result, TMCS charging demand emerges from users’ individual choices.

This is more realistic than assuming that all potential EV demand assigned to a location must automatically be served by the TMCS.

8. Why user choice matters for TMCS scheduling

Suppose a TMCS raises its charging price.

Two effects may occur simultaneously:

  • revenue per kWh increases;
  • fewer EV drivers choose the TMCS.

Similarly, a TMCS may move closer to a demand hotspot and reduce queuing time, making it more attractive relative to an FCS.

Therefore:

TMCS location → waiting time → user choice → charging demand → TMCS schedule.

This creates a coupling between transportation behavior and energy scheduling.

9. Spatial-temporal coordination: the extended graph model

The TMCS cannot instantaneously jump between service locations.

Its physical movement must therefore be modeled explicitly.

The paper constructs an extended graph with two basic arc types:

  • parking arcs: the TMCS remains at a node;
  • transit arcs: the TMCS moves between nodes.

The arcs are further classified according to service type:

  • charging-service arcs \(Z_c\);
  • energy-arbitrage arcs \(Z_a\);
  • exchange arcs \(Z_e\).

This graph keeps track of:

  • where the TMCS is;
  • where it can move next;
  • whether it stays at a charging node;
  • whether it stays at an arbitrage node;
  • travel feasibility between consecutive time intervals.

10. Why the extended graph is useful

Without explicit spatial constraints, an optimizer might produce physically impossible schedules such as:

Hour t:     serve EVs at node A
Hour t+1:   discharge to grid at distant node B

without accounting for travel time.

The extended graph prevents this.

It also prevents immediate unrealistic round trips and enforces initial/terminal positions.

Therefore the scheduling problem is not simply a battery dispatch problem.

It is a coupled:

routing + location + charging-service + storage-dispatch problem.

11. Power and SOC constraints

When TMCS is in energy-arbitrage mode, it may:

  • charge from the grid;
  • discharge to the grid.

When it is in EV charging-service mode, it discharges its battery to EV users.

The model imposes:

  • charging-power limits;
  • discharging-power limits;
  • mutually exclusive charging/discharging states;
  • charging-service power limits;
  • minimum service-quality constraints;
  • SOC upper/lower bounds;
  • inter-temporal SOC dynamics.

The parameter \(\rho\) is particularly important.

It represents the operator’s preference for satisfying EV charging demand and prevents energy arbitrage from excessively sacrificing the primary charging-service function.

12. Objective: maximize Charging Facility Operator profit

The Charging Facility Operator (CFO) maximizes:

\[ \text{Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{O\&M cost} - \text{battery degradation cost} \]

Revenue includes:

  • EV charging-service revenue;
  • grid discharging revenue from arbitrage;
  • minus electricity purchased for arbitrage charging.

O&M cost includes:

  • travel-energy cost;
  • labor cost;
  • maintenance cost.

Battery degradation cost includes both:

  • cycling degradation related to charging/discharging throughput;
  • calendar degradation.

So arbitrage is not treated as free extra revenue.

It consumes battery life and can increase operational cost.

13. Why a deterministic MILP is not enough

If all future EV charging demand were known perfectly, the entire problem could be solved as a multi-period MILP.

But in reality:

future charging demand is uncertain and continuously revealed.

A deterministic full-horizon solution therefore relies on information that is not actually available online.

Traditional alternatives include:

  • stochastic programming;
  • robust optimization.

The paper argues that these methods are relatively static because their decisions are tied to predefined probability distributions or uncertainty sets.

The authors instead want the scheduler to use newly revealed real-time information continuously.

14. MDP reformulation

The multi-period scheduling problem is reformulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).

The state includes key information such as:

  • TMCS SOC;
  • previous-period location/arc state;
  • current EV charging demand.

The decision includes:

  • grid charging power;
  • grid discharging power;
  • EV charging-service power;
  • routing/arc decisions;
  • charging/discharging mode variables.

The exogenous information includes errors or changes in:

  • EV charging demand;
  • TMCS charging-service fee;
  • grid charging price;
  • grid discharging price.

The state transition then connects today’s decision with tomorrow’s feasible state.

15. Why dynamic programming is conceptually correct but computationally difficult

The Bellman equation has the usual structure:

\[ V_t(S_t) = \max_{x_t\in X_t} \left\{ f_t(S_t,x_t) + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}[V_{t+\Delta t}(S_{t+\Delta t})] \right\} \]

This equation makes the core issue explicit:

current profit is not enough; the future value of the resulting SOC/location state must also be considered.

However, exact dynamic programming suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Enumerating all combinations of:

  • SOC;
  • locations;
  • routes;
  • service modes;
  • demand realizations;

is not practical for online operation.

16. Core method: LRH-VFA

The authors therefore combine three ideas:

  1. Value Function Approximation (VFA)
    Approximate future profit rather than solving the full Bellman recursion exactly.

  2. Lookahead optimization
    Optimize several future periods using short-term forecasts.

  3. Rolling horizon
    Execute only the current decision, receive updated information, and solve again.

This combination is called:

Lookahead Rolling Horizon-Value Function Approximation (LRH-VFA).

17. The intuition of the value function

This is the most important part of the paper.

Suppose two candidate decisions give the same current-hour profit.

Decision A

Leaves the TMCS with:

  • high SOC;
  • a useful location close to future charging demand.

Decision B

Leaves the TMCS with:

  • low SOC;
  • a poor location.

A myopic optimizer may treat A and B as equally attractive.

LRH-VFA does not.

It assigns a future value to the post-decision state.

In simple terms:

current decision
     ↓
post-decision SOC/location
     ↓
estimated future profit potential
     ↓
current optimization

So the model learns that stored energy has an opportunity value.

18. Why the value function is approximated mainly through SOC

The paper notes that uncertainty in charging demand ultimately affects how much energy remains in the TMCS battery.

For storage-type optimization, a convex piecewise-linear function can approximate the future value associated with SOC efficiently.

The approximate terminal value is represented using a Piecewise Linear Function (PLF).

Its segment slopes indicate approximately:

how much additional future CFO profit can be obtained from one additional unit of stored energy.

Therefore, the slope is essentially a learned marginal value of stored energy.

19. Offline training stage

The first stage is not day-ahead dispatch.

It is offline learning.

Historical data and Monte Carlo scenarios are used repeatedly.

For each training iteration:

  1. generate a charging-demand scenario;
  2. solve the lookahead scheduling problem;
  3. observe the gradient / marginal value of SOC;
  4. update the PLF slope;
  5. preserve the monotonic/convex structure;
  6. repeat until slopes converge.

The resulting PLFs contain empirical knowledge about how current battery decisions affect future profitability.

This is why the authors describe the model as embedding historical experience into optimization.

20. Online operation stage

After offline training, the PLF slopes are fixed for online operation.

At every time step:

  1. obtain short-term charging-demand predictions over the next \(H\) periods;
  2. solve the LRH-VFA optimization;
  3. calculate decisions for the entire short lookahead window;
  4. execute only the first-period decision;
  5. observe updated demand/state information;
  6. roll the horizon forward and solve again.

This is conceptually similar to receding-horizon control:

forecast → optimize → execute first action → update → repeat

but the terminal state is strengthened by a learned value function rather than being treated as valueless.

21. LRH-VFA versus ordinary rolling horizon

A normal rolling-horizon model looks only \(H\) steps ahead.

Anything after the horizon is effectively ignored.

This can produce a terminal effect.

For example, near the end of the lookahead window, the optimizer may discharge too much battery because it does not see demand after the window.

LRH-VFA adds:

\[ \gamma V_x(S_{terminal}) \]

so the terminal SOC still has economic value.

Therefore:

RH = short-term forecast value
VFA = learned long-term value
LRH-VFA = short-term forecast + learned long-term value

This is the main methodological contribution.

27. Why myopic and RH strategies lose performance

Myopic strategy

It only asks:

What action earns the most profit now?

It may consume battery energy or move to a location that harms future charging service.

Rolling horizon

It sees several future periods, but cannot see beyond the finite window.

A charging-demand surge just outside the lookahead horizon may still be missed.

VFA

It captures long-term value but does not exploit detailed short-term forecast information as effectively.

LRH-VFA

Combines both:

near-term prediction + long-term learned value.

That combination produces the best results.

The paper also examines different EV-growth rates.

  • 112% under medium EV growth;
  • 27% under high EV growth;

in the early operating years considered.

As EV adoption increases, however:

  • more TMCS capacity is required for charging service;
  • less spare capacity remains for arbitrage;
  • existing TMCS fleets can eventually become insufficient.

So arbitrage is particularly valuable during the early/underutilized stage of infrastructure deployment.

31. Charging price and electricity-price spread matter

The economic attractiveness of the two service provisions depends strongly on their relative prices.

If the TMCS charging fee is too low:

  • many EV users choose TMCS;
  • direct charging revenue per kWh falls;
  • arbitrage opportunities shrink;
  • service demand may exceed TMCS capacity.

If the charging fee is too high:

  • too few EV users choose TMCS;
  • charging-service utilization falls;
  • energy arbitrage alone may not fully compensate.

Therefore an intermediate charging-price range is preferable.

Similarly, a larger peak-valley electricity-price spread makes energy arbitrage more attractive.

This can cause the optimizer to deliberately leave more EV charging demand to FCSs while using TMCS battery capacity for grid arbitrage.

32. Pricing volatility creates another uncertainty source

The paper also tests stochastic fluctuations in:

  • EV charging fees;
  • peak electricity prices;
  • valley electricity prices.

Low, medium and high volatility are considered.

The scheduling strategy remains relatively robust under moderate volatility.

However, high volatility increases the optimality gap and reduces profit.

This suggests an obvious extension:

online price learning / adaptive forecasting + LRH-VFA.

33. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Mobile charging infrastructure should be treated as a multi-service asset

A TMCS is not only an emergency EV charger.

Its battery can also provide grid services when charging demand is low.

Lesson 2 — The opportunity cost of stored energy matters

A kWh stored in the truck has different future value depending on:

  • time;
  • location;
  • expected EV demand;
  • electricity price.

This is exactly what the value function tries to capture.

Lesson 3 — Short-term forecasting and long-term learning are complementary

A short lookahead is useful for immediate demand changes.

Historical learning is useful for what lies beyond the forecast window.

LRH-VFA combines both.

Lesson 4 — Utilization can matter as much as nominal charging capacity

Building high-capacity mobile infrastructure for rare demand peaks can be uneconomic unless idle periods are monetized.

Lesson 5 — Mobility couples transportation and electricity markets

The value of a TMCS depends not only on when it charges/discharges but also where it is located.

So spatial decisions are fundamental.

34. Limitations and points to read critically

Important limitations include:

  • EV users are assumed to choose charging options through a rational MNL framework;
  • route choice is simplified using shortest-path assumptions;
  • the main time resolution is one hour, so fast operational dynamics are not represented;
  • traffic and charging-demand generation still rely on simulation assumptions and historical fitting;
  • battery energy consumption per kilometer and charging/discharging efficiencies are assumed constant;
  • electricity-market participation is represented mainly through energy arbitrage rather than a full market-clearing model;
  • competition/cooperation among multiple CFOs is not modeled;
  • ancillary-service participation is left for future work;
  • high prediction/price uncertainty still degrades the performance of LRH-VFA;
  • the theoretical optimality gap of LRH-VFA is difficult to derive, so performance is evaluated empirically against the MILP benchmark.

Two details are also worth checking carefully when reproducing the model.

MNL sign convention

Equation (5) is printed using \(\exp(c_i(j))\), while \(c_i(j)\) is described as a generalized cost.

In a conventional cost-based MNL formulation, higher cost would normally reduce choice probability, often through a negative cost coefficient or negative utility transformation.

So the sign/utility convention should be verified carefully before implementation.

Unit interpretation of dropped charging demand

TABLE II reports charging energy in kWh, while the discussion refers to the 226-unit reduction as 226 kW.

Because the operational interval is one hour, the numerical values coincide, but the energy/power distinction should be kept clear when reusing the result.

注记[P021] Tao et al. (2023) — Customer-Centered Pricing Strategy Based on Privacy-Preserving Load Disaggregation

Journal: IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 14(5), 3401–3412, 2023
DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2023.3238029
Main topics: demand response; thermostatically controlled loads (TCLs); non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM); sequence-to-now learning; privacy preservation; distributed learning; DR potential estimation; price elasticity; bi-level pricing; local transactive energy and capacity markets; CVaR

Quick takeaway

This paper connects load disaggregation with retail electricity pricing.

The key question is:

Can a retailer infer customers’ flexible-load behavior from smart-meter data, preserve customer privacy, and then use the inferred demand-response potential to formulate better DR prices?

The proposed framework has three main layers:

  1. Privacy-preserving NILM separates thermostatically controlled load (TCL) consumption from whole-house smart-meter readings.
  2. Data-driven DR potential and price responsiveness estimation converts NILM outputs into a customer-response model.
  3. A bi-level pricing model uses that response model to optimize the retailer’s retail price, energy-market participation, capacity-market participation, and risk.

The main conceptual contribution is not NILM alone. It is the complete chain:

smart-meter data → appliance-level flexibility → customer response → market bidding → optimal retail pricing.

1. Problem addressed

Price-based demand response requires the retailer to know how customers will react to electricity prices.

Conventional formulations often assume that the retailer knows each customer’s:

  • demand elasticity;
  • utility function;
  • cost function;
  • comfort preference;
  • flexible-load limits.

These assumptions are difficult to satisfy in practice.

The paper therefore proposes a data-driven alternative:

instead of assuming the customer’s flexibility model is known, infer it from historical smart-meter and contextual data.

The focus is on thermostatically controlled loads (TCLs) because they have thermal inertia and can shift or reduce consumption while maintaining indoor comfort.

2. Why TCLs are suitable DR resources

The paper begins from the standard thermal-dynamics logic of a heating load:

\[ T^{in}_{t+1} = T^{in}_{t}e^{-1/RC} + \left(RP^H_t+T^O_t\right) \left(1-e^{-1/RC}\right) \]

subject to a comfort band:

\[ T^H_t-\delta \leq T^{in}_t \leq T^H_t+\delta. \]

The important point is that TCL demand is not perfectly rigid.

Its operation can be adjusted by changing:

  • thermostat setpoint;
  • operating time;
  • power level;

while still satisfying a comfort range.

This gives TCLs scheduling elasticity, which can be converted into DR capability.

3. Overall NILM-integrated pricing framework

The framework uses historical information including:

  • renewable-energy generation;
  • wholesale electricity prices;
  • smart-meter data;
  • environmental-state data;
  • customer-characteristic data.

The data are used in two directions.

First, the retailer uses NILM to estimate:

  • TCL usage;
  • DR potential;
  • price elasticity.

Second, scenario information is used in a bi-level optimization model that represents:

  • customer response;
  • market clearing;
  • retailer pricing.

The final output is the retailer’s optimal customer-centered DR price.

4. What “customer-centered pricing” means here

The phrase does not simply mean offering different tariffs to different households.

Its deeper meaning is:

the pricing strategy is formulated based on the observed usage behavior and estimated flexibility of customers rather than relying only on assumed analytical response models.

The retailer tries to estimate the true DR level of customers before choosing prices.

The paper therefore shifts from:

assumed rational response

toward

data-inferred response capability.

5. NILM: from total smart-meter load to TCL load

Non-intrusive load monitoring decomposes the total smart-meter reading into appliance-level consumption.

For this study, the important decomposition is:

\[ \text{Whole-house load} = \text{Baseload} + \text{TCL load}. \]

The paper treats:

  • baseload as inelastic;
  • TCL load as elastic and available for DR.

This makes NILM a bridge between raw smart-meter data and the flexibility model required by the pricing optimizer.

6. Sequence-to-now (S2N) rather than S2S or S2P

The paper develops a sequence-to-now (S2N) architecture.

Previous NILM approaches include:

  • sequence-to-sequence (S2S): map a sequence of aggregate load to a sequence of appliance load;
  • sequence-to-point (S2P): predict the midpoint appliance value of a window.

The proposed S2N instead predicts the latest/current appliance reading using the current rolling input window:

\[ x_{t+W-1} = \Phi(Y_{t:t+W-1})+\tau. \]

This is important for online monitoring because it avoids depending on future smart-meter readings to estimate the current state.

The authors therefore position S2N as more suitable for real-time DR applications.

7. Multi-type features instead of smart-meter readings only

A major design choice is that the neural network does not use only aggregate meter readings.

Three feature channels are used.

Channel 1 — Smart-meter readings

These contain the aggregate household electricity signal.

Channel 2 — Environmental states

The selected variables include:

  • outdoor temperature;
  • moisture;
  • precipitation;
  • wind speed;
  • atmospheric pressure.

This is especially important for TCLs because weather strongly affects heating/cooling demand.

Channel 3 — Customer characteristic identifications

The paper includes 13 characteristics from three groups:

  • occupant information;
  • dwelling properties;
  • appliance properties.

Examples include age, employment status, number of children, number of residents, home type, ownership, bedroom number, building age, and appliance counts.

The intuition is straightforward:

households with similar aggregate power traces may still have different flexible-load behavior because their weather sensitivity and household characteristics differ.

8. Multi-channel CNN-LSTM architecture

The three feature types are processed separately because they have different structures.

For meter readings and environmental states:

CNN blocks → LSTM blocks

are used to extract local and temporal features.

For customer characteristics:

fully connected layers

are used because these features do not form a time series.

The channels are then merged and passed through fully connected layers to estimate the current TCL power.

The rolling window is 24 h.

At a 1-minute smart-meter resolution, the raw meter-input dimension is therefore 1440 × 1.

PCA reduces this to 512 × 1 before it enters the neural network.

9. Why privacy is a central issue

A NILM model can reveal much more than total electricity consumption.

Detailed smart-meter and household-characteristic data can potentially expose:

  • occupancy patterns;
  • appliance-use habits;
  • household routines;
  • socio-economic characteristics.

A centralized deep-learning scheme would require customers’ raw data to be collected and stored by a central entity.

The paper therefore introduces privacy protection at two stages:

  1. feature extraction;
  2. model training.

10. Privacy layer 1: encrypted distributed PCA

PCA is used to reduce the meter-data dimension.

However, directly calculating a global covariance matrix would normally require pooling all customer data.

The paper decomposes the covariance calculation across multiple retailers.

Each retailer computes local intermediate matrices, encrypts them, and passes only encrypted aggregated information through the retailer chain.

The final retailer sends the encrypted sums to the server.

The server uses its private key to recover only the aggregated quantities needed to calculate the covariance matrix and principal components.

The important privacy logic is:

  • the server does not directly receive each retailer’s raw customer data;
  • one retailer cannot decrypt another retailer’s private data;
  • all retailers can still obtain a common principal-component basis.

11. Privacy layer 2: distributed collaborative learning

The neural-network training is also decentralized.

Each retailer:

  • keeps its private local dataset;
  • downloads selected global parameters;
  • performs local stochastic-gradient updates;
  • uploads only part of the gradient/parameter information.

The central server maintains the latest global parameters.

This is conceptually similar to distributed collaborative learning and shares the privacy motivation of federated-learning architectures, although the paper describes its own selective upload/download procedure rather than simply applying standard FedAvg.

The key idea is:

share learning information, not raw customer data.

12. Privacy protection comes with an accuracy cost

The case study explicitly shows a privacy-performance trade-off.

With more training agents/retailers, each agent receives a smaller share of the fixed dataset.

The reported NILM performance gradually deteriorates.

For example, as the number of retailers increases from 1 to 5:

  • MAE rises from 0.140 to 0.194;
  • RMSE rises from 0.026 to 0.064;
  • EpD rises from 8.31 to 9.38;
  • \(R^2\) falls from 0.988 to 0.955.

However, distributed training remains much better than the standalone case in which each retailer trains independently without cooperation.

So the paper’s message is not that privacy is free.

It is:

modest learning-performance loss can buy a meaningful reduction in raw-data sharing.

13. From NILM to DR potential: changing-point model

After separating the TCL load, the paper estimates the relationship between TCL consumption and outdoor temperature.

For a heating system:

\[ P^H_{i,n,t} = \beta^H_{i,n} \max\left(T^H_{i,n,t}-T^O_t,0\right) +\varepsilon_{i,n}. \]

Here:

  • \(P^H_{i,n,t}\): TCL power obtained from NILM;
  • \(T^H_{i,n,t}\): thermostat setpoint;
  • \(T^O_t\): outdoor temperature;
  • \(\beta^H_{i,n}\): temperature-response slope.

Historical TCL consumption and outdoor temperature are used to regress the household’s temperature-response behavior.

The reported median temperature-response slope is approximately:

77.5 W/°C.

A larger slope generally means stronger temperature sensitivity and therefore greater DR potential.

14. Estimating DR potential

The paper defines the TCL DR potential by comparing the preferred thermostat setting with a minimum acceptable indoor temperature.

Conceptually:

\[ \text{DR potential} \approx \text{temperature sensitivity} \times \text{available comfort adjustment}. \]

A household with:

  • high temperature sensitivity;
  • a large allowable comfort band;

can provide more flexible load reduction than a less sensitive household.

The case study reports that the estimated DR potential is close to the ground truth, with a tendency toward slight under-estimation.

The authors argue that conservative under-estimation is preferable to over-estimation because exaggerated flexibility could lead the retailer to promise DR capacity that customers cannot actually deliver.

15. Price responsiveness is also estimated from data

The paper does not stop at estimating technical flexibility.

It also models how strongly a customer changes TCL demand in response to price.

The response model uses:

  • DR potential;
  • price elasticity;
  • actual retail DR price;
  • nominal retail price.

The final customer-response expression combines:

baseline temperature-driven TCL demand − price-induced DR adjustment.

The elasticity coefficient is estimated from historical data through an error-correction model.

So there are two distinct quantities:

  • DR potential: how much flexibility physically exists;
  • price elasticity: how much of that flexibility the customer is willing to activate in response to a price signal.

This distinction is important.

16. Bi-level pricing model

The pricing problem is formulated from three perspectives:

  • retailer;
  • customers;
  • system operator.

The retailer is the upper-level decision maker.

The lower level contains:

  1. customer response to the retailer’s DR price;
  2. system-operator market clearing.

This captures the feedback loop:

retail price → customer demand → market clearing → market price → retailer profit → retail price.

17. Upper-level objective: retailer profit with risk control

The retailer maximizes expected profit.

The main economic components are:

Revenue from customers

Electricity is sold to residential customers at the DR retail price.

Capacity-market revenue

The retailer aggregates TCL flexibility and offers reserve capacity to the local capacity market.

Energy-purchase costs

The retailer purchases electricity from:

  • the local transactive market;
  • the wholesale market.

Reliability cost

Load curtailment is penalized.

Risk term

The model includes Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR).

This means the retailer is not optimized only for expected profit.

It also penalizes unfavorable tail outcomes.

So the upper-level problem balances:

profitability ↔︎ reliability ↔︎ risk.

18. Lower-level customer problem is not a conventional optimization model

In many DR studies, every customer solves an explicit cost-minimization problem.

The retailer would therefore need to know the customer’s:

  • utility function;
  • disutility function;
  • thermal parameters;
  • comfort cost;
  • demand elasticity.

This paper deliberately avoids that structure.

The customer-response model is instead reconstructed from NILM and historical data.

This is one of the paper’s strongest methodological ideas:

replace hard-to-obtain private behavioral models with observable data-driven response models.

19. Lower-level market clearing: joint energy and capacity market

The local transactive market jointly clears:

  • energy;
  • reserve capacity.

Generators offer energy and capacity.

The retailer:

  • bids to purchase energy;
  • offers TCL-based reserve capacity.

The reserved TCL capacity is explicitly bounded by the estimated DR potential.

This makes NILM accuracy economically important.

If flexibility is over-estimated, the retailer may over-offer reserve.

If it is under-estimated, profitable capacity-market opportunities may be lost.

20. Distribution-network constraints are included

The lower-level market-clearing model is not purely economic.

It includes distribution-network constraints for:

  • active-power balance;
  • reactive-power balance;
  • nodal voltage;
  • active-power line limits;
  • reactive-power line limits.

Therefore the retailer’s DR pricing can indirectly affect network operation and congestion through the customers’ TCL response.

This is why the paper later reports both retailer-level and system-level benefits.

21. Case-study setup

The NILM model is tested using public data from:

  • Pecan Street;
  • Irish CER smart-meter dataset.

Customers are mapped to the IEEE 33-bus distribution system.

Market signals and clearing results are taken from the Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO) dataset.

The reported computational settings include:

  • NILM training time: 41.3 min;
  • optimization time: 4.2 min;
  • market/settlement interval used in the study: 15 min.

The NILM training is offline, so its longer training time is considered acceptable by the authors.

22. TCL demand aligns with an important evening DR period

The daily TCL-consumption distribution peaks approximately during:

15:00–20:00.

This period overlaps with:

  • declining solar generation;
  • rising residential demand.

Therefore TCLs are particularly useful for evening peak management.

This is reflected later in the optimized DR price, which rises strongly in the evening.

23. NILM accuracy: proposed S2N model performs best in the reported comparison

The proposed S2N CNN-LSTM architecture is compared with:

  • AlexNet;
  • deep autoencoder (dAE);
  • GRU;
  • S2P CNN.

The reported metrics for the proposed model are:

  • MAE: 0.140;
  • RMSE: 0.026;
  • EpD: 8.31;
  • \(R^2\): 0.988.

The paper attributes the improvement mainly to the inclusion of:

  • environmental states;
  • customer characteristics;

in addition to meter readings.

The figures also show that the multi-feature model follows TCL peaks more closely than the meter-only model.

24. Important reporting issue in Table II

There is a metric-consistency issue worth flagging before reusing the reported values.

For the proposed model, Table II reports:

  • MAE = 0.140;
  • RMSE = 0.026.

Under the standard definitions of MAE and RMSE, calculated on the same observations and in the same units, RMSE should not be smaller than MAE.

The same pattern also appears for some benchmark models.

Therefore, one of the following may be true:

  • the metrics are normalized differently;
  • the table labels are inconsistent;
  • the values were reported using different scaling;
  • there is a typographical/reporting error.

The paper itself does not resolve this point.

So these values should be quoted as reported by the authors, rather than interpreted as directly comparable under standard error definitions without further verification.

25. NILM-integrated pricing is close to the fully model-based ideal case

The paper compares three pricing cases:

Base case

No optimized DR program; TOU pricing is used.

NILM-integrated pricing

Customer flexibility and response are inferred from data.

Fully model-based pricing

The retailer is assumed to know the complete customer-response model.

The optimized NILM-integrated retail price is very close to the fully model-based price.

Both strategies increase price during the evening peak and reduce TCL demand compared with the TOU base case.

This is the paper’s main practical argument:

a data-driven method with weaker information assumptions can approach the economic performance of an idealized full-information model.

The reported comparison is:

27. Better NILM improves both retailer and grid outcomes

The paper then replaces the proposed NILM model with weaker NILM benchmarks.

The trend is consistent:

more accurate NILM tends to produce:

  • lower retailer energy-purchase cost;
  • higher retailer revenue;
  • lower reliability cost;
  • improved risk performance;
  • lower system energy-production cost;
  • lower congestion cost.

This demonstrates an important cross-layer effect:

prediction quality → flexibility estimation → pricing quality → market outcome → network outcome.

NILM accuracy is therefore not only an AI metric.

It has measurable economic and power-system consequences.

28. What this paper teaches

Lesson 1 — Load disaggregation becomes more valuable when linked to a decision problem

The paper does not stop at reporting NILM accuracy.

It converts NILM outputs into:

  • DR potential;
  • price elasticity;
  • reserve capacity;
  • retail pricing;
  • market bids.

This is much stronger than treating NILM as an isolated prediction task.

Lesson 2 — Data-driven models can replace some strong behavioral assumptions

The retailer does not need perfect knowledge of every customer’s utility and cost functions.

Historical smart-meter and contextual data can provide a practical response model.

Lesson 3 — Technical flexibility and behavioral responsiveness are different

A household may technically be able to reduce TCL load but may respond weakly to price.

Both DR potential and price elasticity matter.

Lesson 4 — Privacy has an economic-performance trade-off

Distributed learning protects raw information but reduces prediction accuracy as data become more fragmented.

Lesson 5 — AI prediction quality should be evaluated by downstream system value

A better NILM model improves not only MAE/RMSE-type metrics but also retailer profit, reliability cost, production cost, and congestion cost.

Lesson 6 — Flexible demand can participate in multiple value streams

The retailer earns from both:

  • selling retail energy;
  • providing capacity using aggregated TCL flexibility.

29. Limitations

Important limitations include:

  • the study focuses mainly on TCLs, while other flexible appliances are treated less explicitly;
  • the customer-response model is still a fitted reduced-order model rather than a fully adaptive behavioral model;
  • distributed learning sacrifices prediction performance as the number of participating retailers increases;
  • the privacy framework is evaluated mainly through architectural privacy arguments and prediction accuracy rather than a formal differential-privacy budget;
  • the proposed NILM still has monitoring latency;
  • customer habits and environmental relationships may change over time, but the trained model is mainly offline;
  • the pricing formulation assumes the proposed local transactive energy/capacity market structure;
  • wholesale-market participation is not modeled in the same detail as the local transactive market;
  • retail competition is represented through price constraints rather than an explicit multi-retailer strategic game;
  • the paper does not deeply study strategic misreporting or adversarial behavior by customers/retailers;
  • the Table II error metrics appear to contain a reporting/scaling inconsistency that should be checked before direct reuse.

The authors explicitly identify low-latency NILM and online learning as important future directions.
